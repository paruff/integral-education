## AQAL Competency Map

This map defines measurable competencies for all quadrants, levels, lines, states, and types. Use it as the canonical design reference for pathway, assessment, and analytics decisions.

**Status:** Draft — awaiting SME validation (see [SME Review Gate](#sme-review-gate)).

## How to Read This Map

- Competencies are organized by AQAL dimension.
- Each competency domain includes level-by-level progression indicators, observable behaviors, and evidence signals.
- Evidence signals are listed as both qualitative and quantitative where applicable.
- Levels are progression bands, not rigid identity labels. Learners may operate at different levels across different domains.
- States and types modulate how competencies are expressed; they do not change the core competency structure.
- **Important distinction:** The L1–L4 rows throughout this map are self-assessable rubrics designed for Amber altitude and above, where reflective self-evaluation is a stable capacity. The [Pre-Conventional Stage Descriptive Reference](#pre-conventional-stage-descriptive-reference) section (Magic/Purple and Magic-Mythic/Red) uses a different format — descriptive facilitator-reference rows, not learner self-assessment rubrics.

## Developmental Levels (Platform Working Model)

| Code | Name | Characteristic Capacity |
|---|---|---|
| `L1` | Foundational | Basic awareness; can name concepts and comply with guidance in familiar contexts |
| `L2` | Differentiated | Distinguishes and applies concepts independently in familiar and moderately novel contexts |
| `L3` | Integrative | Coordinates multiple perspectives, methods, and constraints simultaneously |
| `L4` | Transformative | Adapts the system, teaches others, and generates novel protocols across complex contexts |

These levels map to conventional developmental terminology as follows:

| Platform Level | Developmental Analogy | Spiral Dynamics Rough Equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | Preconventional → Conventional entry | Red → Amber |
| L2 | Conventional → Post-conventional entry | Amber → Orange |
| L3 | Post-conventional | Orange → Green |
| L4 | Integral and beyond | Teal → Turquoise |

## Quadrant Competency Domains

### Quadrant I — Interior-Individual: Self-Awareness and Regulation

**Domain statement:** The capacity to observe, name, and constructively work with one's own internal states, emotions, values, and developmental edges.

#### Progression Indicators

| Level | Progression Indicator | Observable Behaviors |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | Names present internal state using basic emotion vocabulary | Identifies broad emotion categories (happy, sad, anxious, angry) in real time; uses a provided vocabulary list without prompting; reports state before and after a practice session |
| L2 | Uses regulation tools in routine and moderately stressful contexts | Selects and applies at least two distinct regulation strategies (e.g., breath, labeling, body scan) without coaching; describes why the chosen tool fits the context; shows measurable reduction in self-reported distress within a session |
| L3 | Chooses state-shifting practice contextually and adapts when the first approach fails | Articulates which quadrant, line, or state dimension is most activated; switches strategies mid-session when the first approach is ineffective; integrates insight about recurring patterns into a personal practice plan |
| L4 | Designs and teaches state-aware practice for others | Creates individualized practice sequences for peers or students; incorporates contraindications; documents protocol rationale; evaluates outcomes and adjusts the protocol based on evidence |

#### Evidence Signals

| Level | Qualitative Evidence | Quantitative Evidence | Assessment Cues / Artifacts | Real-Context Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Reflection journal entries name at least 3 distinct emotion states per week | Emotion vocabulary breadth: ≥ 5 distinct terms over 4 entries | Self-report check-in form; facilitator observation checklist | Learner writes "I noticed anxiety in my chest before the meeting" |
| L2 | Reflection describes strategy selection rationale; post-practice entries show before/after contrast | Self-reported distress rating decreases ≥ 2 points (1–10 scale) within session; regulation tool use logged ≥ 3 times per week | Practice log with timestamps; audio/video of practice session reviewed by facilitator | Learner selects box breathing before a high-stakes conversation and logs the outcome |
| L3 | Personal practice plan references multiple AQAL dimensions; learner describes pivots in strategy during session | Practice adherence ≥ 80% over 4-week window; at least 2 documented strategy pivots per month | Written practice plan reviewed against protocol template; facilitator debrief notes | Learner identifies somatic tension as "It" quadrant signal, shifts to body-scan practice, then integrates insight in a "We" conversation |
| L4 | Peer or student outcomes traceable to learner-designed protocol | Protocol adoption rate among peers ≥ 60%; at least one measurable outcome improvement in mentee self-reports | Protocol document; peer outcome log; presentation or workshop facilitation recording | Learner designs a 4-week mindfulness onboarding sequence for a team and documents team-level wellbeing changes |

### Quadrant We — Interior-Collective: Perspective-Taking and Dialogue

**Domain statement:** The capacity to recognize, engage with, and integrate multiple subjective viewpoints within a shared cultural space.

#### Progression Indicators

| Level | Progression Indicator | Observable Behaviors |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | Identifies at least two valid perspectives on a shared topic | Restates another person's view accurately without evaluation; acknowledges that more than one perspective can be legitimate; paraphrases positions before disagreeing |
| L2 | Engages in respectful disagreement and collaborative inquiry | Asks clarifying questions before countering; maintains a non-defensive posture during disagreement; distinguishes between a person's position and their underlying interests |
| L3 | Synthesizes opposing views into a hybrid or integrating position | Produces a written or verbal synthesis that both parties recognize as fair; identifies shared values beneath surface disagreement; facilitates a dialogue that moves from debate to inquiry |
| L4 | Facilitates plural dialogue with measurable learning or relational gains | Designs and runs structured dialogue processes; measures participant perspective shift pre/post; manages group-level dynamics including power and silence |

#### Evidence Signals

| Level | Qualitative Evidence | Quantitative Evidence | Assessment Cues / Artifacts | Real-Context Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Forum posts paraphrase peer positions accurately before responding | Paraphrase accuracy rated ≥ 3/4 by a peer reviewer | Forum post analysis; peer paraphrase review rubric | Learner restates a peer's argument about systems thinking before offering a counter-view |
| L2 | Dialogue transcripts show clarifying questions and interest-exploration moves | ≥ 2 clarifying questions per dialogue; zero ad-hominem markers in coded transcript | Dialogue transcript coded for discourse quality; facilitator checklist | Learner asks "What outcome matters most to you here?" before disagreeing in a team decision meeting |
| L3 | Synthesis document integrates at least three perspectives with explicit common-ground identification | Peer ratings of synthesis quality ≥ 4/5; both "sides" of a dialogue rate the synthesis as fair | Written synthesis reviewed against integration rubric; dialogue recording | Learner facilitates a team discussion on values conflict and produces a written integration that all parties endorse |
| L4 | Post-dialogue surveys show measurable perspective shift among participants | Mean perspective shift score ≥ 1.5 SD from baseline; facilitator rubric score ≥ 85% | Facilitator evaluation rubric; pre/post perspective survey; group learning artifact | Learner co-designs a structured dialogue protocol for a cross-cultural cohort and evaluates its effectiveness over three sessions |

### Quadrant It — Exterior-Individual: Skillful Behavior and Execution

**Domain statement:** The capacity to perform specific, observable, repeatable behaviors with increasing independence, precision, and adaptability.

#### Progression Indicators

| Level | Progression Indicator | Observable Behaviors |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | Performs a defined behavior with guidance | Follows a step-by-step protocol when prompted; completes a behavioral task under direct supervision or with a written checklist; identifies errors when pointed out |
| L2 | Performs independently in stable, familiar scenarios | Executes the behavior without prompting; self-corrects minor errors in real time; explains the rationale for each step |
| L3 | Adapts behavior under novel constraints or competing demands | Modifies the behavioral protocol when conditions change; manages two competing behavioral demands simultaneously; documents adaptations for future reference |
| L4 | Designs repeatable behavioral protocols and trains others | Writes a behavioral protocol from first principles; identifies failure modes and builds in safeguards; trains at least one other person to L2 proficiency |

#### Evidence Signals

| Level | Qualitative Evidence | Quantitative Evidence | Assessment Cues / Artifacts | Real-Context Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Checklist completion with facilitator signature | Task completion rate ≥ 80% with guidance; error rate ≤ 30% on first attempt | Observed performance checklist; skill demonstration rubric | Learner completes a structured listening exercise following the provided protocol step-by-step |
| L2 | Self-correction log documents error recognition and repair | Independent task completion rate ≥ 90%; error rate ≤ 10% across three consecutive attempts | Video-recorded performance review; timed skill assessment | Learner conducts a focused listening session without a checklist and self-corrects a pace error mid-session |
| L3 | Adaptation memo documents constraint and chosen workaround | Successful completion rate under constraint conditions ≥ 75%; adaptation documented for ≥ 2 distinct constraint types | Adaptation memo; observed performance under scenario variation | Learner adapts a feedback conversation protocol when the receiver becomes emotionally activated, documenting the pivots taken |
| L4 | Peer trainer evaluation confirms trainee achieved L2 | Trainee reaches L2 proficiency within 4 sessions; protocol document passes peer review for clarity and safety | Protocol document; trainer observation of trainee session; trainee performance data | Learner writes a 5-step deep listening protocol and trains a colleague to independent competency, evaluated by facilitator observation |

### Quadrant Its — Exterior-Collective: Systems Literacy and Intervention

**Domain statement:** The capacity to understand, map, and intervene in the structural and institutional systems that shape collective behavior and outcomes.

#### Progression Indicators

| Level | Progression Indicator | Observable Behaviors |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | Identifies system components, roles, and basic relationships | Names the key actors, structures, and flows in a given system; identifies at least one feedback dynamic; describes how their role fits within the system |
| L2 | Maps feedback loops, delays, and reinforcing/balancing dynamics | Produces a causal loop diagram for a familiar system; distinguishes reinforcing from balancing loops; predicts likely system behavior given a perturbation |
| L3 | Designs leverage-point interventions informed by system structure | Selects a leverage point with explicit rationale; designs an intervention that addresses a structural driver, not a symptom; anticipates unintended consequences |
| L4 | Evaluates system interventions with outcome data and revises protocols | Defines leading and lagging indicators before implementing; collects and analyzes outcome data; revises intervention based on evidence and communicates findings to stakeholders |

#### Evidence Signals

| Level | Qualitative Evidence | Quantitative Evidence | Assessment Cues / Artifacts | Real-Context Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | System description identifies ≥ 3 components and at least one feedback dynamic | Accuracy rated ≥ 3/4 on a structured rubric by two independent reviewers | System description memo; facilitated review session | Learner maps the feedback loop between team morale, communication quality, and project outcomes in a written memo |
| L2 | Causal loop diagram uses correct notation and distinguishes loop types | Diagram includes ≥ 2 feedback loops; notation accuracy ≥ 85% rated by reviewer | Causal loop diagram with legend; written behavior-over-time description | Learner draws a reinforcing loop of trust → communication → trust in a team setting and predicts what happens when trust breaks |
| L3 | Intervention design document names leverage point, rationale, and anticipated side effects | Leverage point rating ≥ 3/5 on Meadows hierarchy; at least 2 unintended consequences identified | Intervention design memo reviewed against template; peer critique session | Learner proposes changing information flows (Meadows level 6) in an organization's reporting structure to shift decision-making quality |
| L4 | Evaluation report includes pre/post data and revised protocol | Statistical or descriptive evidence of ≥ 1 measurable outcome change; revised protocol documented | Evaluation report; stakeholder presentation; revised intervention protocol | Learner implements a team communication protocol, tracks error rates and morale scores over 8 weeks, and presents a data-informed revision |

## Lines of Development

Each line of development is relatively independent. A learner may be at L3 on cognitive and L1 on somatic. Curriculum design should address the relevant lines explicitly.

### Cognitive Line

**Competency statement:** Uses evidence and reasoning appropriate to the complexity of the task.

| Level | Progression Indicator | Observable Behaviors | Qualitative Evidence | Quantitative Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Identifies a relevant source and states a claim it supports | Names a source type; extracts a main claim; distinguishes fact from opinion at a basic level | Reading log with source and claim identification | ≥ 70% accuracy on a source-evaluation quiz |
| L2 | Evaluates source quality and distinguishes correlation from causation | Applies an evidence-quality checklist; identifies at least one methodological weakness in a given source; avoids causal claims from observational data | Source critique memo using platform checklist | Source critique rated ≥ 3/4 on evidence-quality rubric |
| L3 | Integrates evidence across sources and constructs a reasoned argument | Synthesizes ≥ 3 sources into a coherent argument; acknowledges counter-evidence; labels claim strength appropriately (tentative, probable, established) | Argumentative essay or reasoning memo | Argument quality rated ≥ 4/5 on a structured reasoning rubric |
| L4 | Designs evidence protocols and teaches critical reasoning skills to others | Writes a domain-specific evidence-vetting checklist; facilitates a reasoning calibration session; documents reasoning norms for a team or community | Evidence protocol document; calibration session facilitation recording | Peer application of protocol rated ≥ 80% fidelity |

**Competency statement:** Uses body-based practices to support attention, resilience, and integrated action.

| Level | Progression Indicator | Observable Behaviors | Qualitative Evidence | Quantitative Evidence |

## States Competency Framework

States are temporary experiences of consciousness. Competencies in this dimension concern the capacity to access, stabilize, and integrate state experiences safely and skillfully.

### Focused Attention (Gross/Waking State Optimization)

| Level | Progression Indicator | Observable Behaviors | Evidence Signals | Safety Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Sustains attention on a single task for a defined window | Completes a 5-minute focused practice without shifting to another task; reports attention quality pre/post | Practice log; self-rated attention quality | Avoid practice durations > 10 min at L1; include rest breaks |
| L2 | Recovers attention after distraction without distress | Detects mind-wandering within ≤ 30 seconds; gently redirects without self-criticism; completes a 15-minute session | Distraction-recovery log; session completion rate | Monitor for frustration; provide encouragement cues |
| L3 | Selects attention practice by context and performance goal | Chooses between focused and open practices based on task demands; sustains ≥ 20-minute sessions; uses attention quality as a performance feedback signal | Practice-selection rationale log; performance task score pre/post | Allow flexibility; do not enforce a single technique |
| L4 | Trains others in focused-attention practice and designs personalized protocols | Runs a group attention-training session; individualizes duration and technique; evaluates participant outcomes | Facilitation recording; participant attention improvement data | Include contraindication screening for trauma or dissociation |

## How States and Types Influence Observable Behaviors

### State Influence

State training modulates the baseline competency level accessible to the learner in a given moment:

- **Dysregulated state** (high activation, low coherence): Learners typically access L1 behaviors even when L3 competency has been trained. Assessment during dysregulation underestimates stable capacity.
- **Regulated state** (moderate activation, coherent attention): Learner expresses trained competency level. This is the target condition for summative assessment.
- **Expanded state** (contemplative, insight-active): Learners may temporarily access L4-quality reflection without stable L4 capacity. Insights from these states require integration before they constitute durable competency.

**Design implication:** Include a brief state-check and regulation moment before any high-stakes assessment event. Flag assessments taken in known dysregulated conditions for review.

### Type Influence

Types are stable styles that cut across levels and states. They shape how competencies are expressed and how evidence should be interpreted:

| Type Dimension | How Competency Appears | Assessment Design Adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Introversion-Extraversion | Introverts may show higher-quality competency in written reflection; extraverts in dialogue and group work | Offer both solo and social evidence formats; avoid weighting one mode exclusively |
| Structure-Seeking vs. Exploratory | Structure-seekers perform best with clear rubrics and defined steps; exploratory learners may underperform on rigid tasks but show creative integration | Provide rubrics upfront; allow structured OR emergent demonstration formats for L3+ outcomes |
| Analytical vs. Relational Preference | Analytical learners produce stronger evidence-evaluation artifacts; relational learners produce stronger interpersonal and compassion artifacts | Pair both formats in competency evidence requirements; do not reduce overall competency claim to one mode |
| Time-Constrained Professionals | May demonstrate L3 competency in micro-sessions but under-complete longer practice requirements | Allow micro-format completion paths with equivalent rigor; adjust duration thresholds; require outcome evidence not just time-on-task |

**Design implication:** No single evidence format should be the sole basis for a competency judgment. Provide at least two demonstration modalities per domain (written + performance, or solo + social).

## Pre-Conventional Stage Descriptive Reference

> **Facilitator Reference Only — Not for Learner Self-Assessment**
>
> The L1–L4 progression framework used throughout this map assumes a degree of self-reflective capacity that is not yet stable at the Magic/Purple or Magic-Mythic/Red developmental altitudes. Self-assessment rubrics presuppose that the learner can take their own developmental structure as an object of reflection — a capacity that is itself a product of later-stage development. For this reason, the standard rubric format is inappropriate as a self-assessment instrument for people whose current centre of gravity is at these stages.
>
> The tables below are **facilitator-reference material only**: descriptive patterns that help a practitioner recognize where someone may be centered, design developmentally appropriate support, and honor the genuine gifts present at each stage. These descriptions are not rubrics for learners to score themselves, and they are not intended to be shared directly with someone whose current centre of gravity is at the described stage. Unlike the Amber-and-above rows in this map, they carry no assessment function.

**Cross-references:** [MM-01 · Magic/Purple Stage Orientation](../modules/magic-purple-stage-orientation) | [MM-02 · Magic-Mythic/Red Stage Orientation](../modules/magic-mythic-red-stage-orientation)

### Magic/Purple — Facilitator Descriptive Reference

**Stage overview:** Purple/Magic is characterized by participatory embeddedness, tribal belonging, animistic world-sensing, and pre-individual identity. The self is not yet clearly differentiated from the group. Meaning is generated through ritual, repetition, and mythic story. Safety is experienced as belonging, and the world is perceived as alive, animated, and interpersonally responsive.

See [MM-01 · Magic/Purple Stage Orientation](../modules/magic-purple-stage-orientation) for the full stage-level account.

#### Quadrant Expression at Magic/Purple

| Quadrant | Characteristic Expression | Facilitation Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **I — Interior-Individual** | Self is experienced as inseparable from the tribal field; personal emotion is felt as collective participation rather than individual state. Emotional states are immediate, somatic, and undifferentiated. Fear of separation from the group is the primary interior signal. | Do not ask for individual self-report or introspection in isolation. Embed reflection in collective ritual or shared practice. Emotional vocabulary exercises will not register unless anchored in familiar group context. |
| **We — Interior-Collective** | Shared meaning is maintained through story, ritual, ancestry, and taboo. Belonging is unconditional within the in-group; difference is experienced as threat or spirit-influence. Dialogue is participatory rather than deliberate. | Build shared ritual before introducing content. Trust is established through demonstrated group membership and repeated relational contact, not through individual competence signaling. |
| **It — Exterior-Individual** | Action is guided by embodied habit, imitation, and ritual repetition rather than by explicit instruction or deliberate skill-building. The body participates in collective practice before developing individual behavioral protocols. | Skill-building requires embodied imitation within group context. Written or analytic instruction registers poorly; modeling within the group field is the primary learning mechanism. |
| **Its — Exterior-Collective** | The world is understood as animated and relational: land, ancestors, spirits, weather, and creatures are participants in a shared field. Systems and structures are felt rather than analyzed. | Avoid abstract systems maps or causal analysis. Environmental and collective forces are best approached through story, symbol, and felt sense rather than structural diagrams. |

#### Line Expression at Magic/Purple

| Line | Characteristic Pattern | What Healthy Expression Looks Like |
|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive** | Pre-operational to early concrete. Thinking is animistic, analogical, and participatory. Causality is felt through magical association: adjacent things influence each other; the part stands for the whole; the name is the thing. | Strong narrative intelligence; rich metaphorical and symbolic capacity; receptive to learning through story, imitation, and embodied ritual. Formal-logical analysis is not yet available as a mode. |
| **Emotional** | Emotions are undifferentiated, immediate, and collective. The distinction between "my feeling" and "the group's feeling" is not yet stable. Dysregulation spreads rapidly through the group field. | Co-regulation through physical proximity, shared rhythm, song, and ritual is the primary stabilizing mechanism. Individual affect labeling is not yet reliably accessible; group emotional climate is the functional unit. |
| **Moral** | Pre-conventional: punishment-and-obedience orientation. Right action is what the group's taboo system permits; wrong action is what the elder or ritual authority prohibits. Moral logic is embedded in story and precedent, not articulated principle. | Moral learning happens through story, example, and felt consequence within the group. Abstract principle-stating is not yet the appropriate instructional medium. |
| **Interpersonal** | Attachment is primary; relationship is experienced through co-regulation, proximity, and tribal belonging rather than through negotiated exchange or mutual understanding. People outside the group are not yet fully individuated other-subjects. | Relational attunement and physical co-regulation are primary. Trust requires demonstrated loyalty, ritual participation, and felt belonging — not verbal agreement or contractual assurance. |
| **Somatic** | The body is the primary site of knowing. Somatic states carry meaning before cognitive processing; rhythm, movement, touch, and sensory participation in the environment are the default intelligence channels. | Somatic and movement-based learning is highly appropriate. Body-based group practices (circle, song, dance, breath) are more effective learning vehicles than verbal or written instruction. |
| **Spiritual/Meaning** | The world is experienced as alive and responsive. Meaning flows through participation with the sacred field — ancestors, spirits, natural forces — rather than through individual reflection or doctrinal study. | Ritual and participatory practice are the meaning-making vehicles. Attempts to intellectualize or systematize this stage's spiritual engagement will be experienced as disconnecting rather than deepening. |

#### State Expression at Magic/Purple

| State Domain | How States Are Typically Accessed and Expressed |
|---|---|
| **Gross/Waking** | Somatic presence is high; focused attention as a deliberate individual practice is not yet stable. Group-field attention during ritual is the primary attentional unit. |
| **Mythic/Imaginal** | Vivid access to mythic and imaginal experience: ancestors, spirits, and animated entities populate the field and are experienced as real participants. Dream and waking experience are not sharply distinguished. |
| **Contemplative** | Participatory states of merged awareness with the group, the land, or the sacred field are natural. The individual is not yet sufficiently separate from the field to sustain independent contemplative practice as a subject. |

#### Type Modulation at Magic/Purple

Types (introverted/extraverted, structure-seeking/exploratory, etc.) modulate how the above patterns express, but the stage structure takes precedence: individual style variation is secondary to the collective field at this altitude. Facilitators should not draw individual-level type conclusions about a person without first accounting for the stage context.

### Magic-Mythic/Red — Facilitator Descriptive Reference

**Stage overview:** Red/Magic-Mythic is characterized by emergent individualism, power-orientation, egocentric exchange, impulsive present-orientation, and heroic self-assertion. The self has differentiated from the tribal field but has not yet organized under an internalized moral code beyond raw power and exchange. Courage, vitality, directness, and honest self-assertion are the genuine gifts of this stage.

See [MM-02 · Magic-Mythic/Red Stage Orientation](../modules/magic-mythic-red-stage-orientation) for the full stage-level account.

#### Quadrant Expression at Magic-Mythic/Red

| Quadrant | Characteristic Expression | Facilitation Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **I — Interior-Individual** | Emotions are immediate, intense, and action-generating. Felt sense of personal power is the primary interior organizing signal. Introspection is limited and unstable; SEEKING and RAGE systems are strongly activated. | Introspective formats will not register well unless framed in terms of power, strength, and personal relevance. Use active and embodied reflection rather than written analysis. Avoid requesting vulnerability or nuanced self-appraisal — these require capacities not yet available at this altitude. |
| **We — Interior-Collective** | Others are sorted rapidly into ally or obstacle, strong or weak. Group membership is primarily strategic. Loyalty extends only as far as power relationships maintain it. Shared meaning is built through shared action and victory, not through dialogue or deliberate inquiry. | Trust is established through demonstrated strength and reliable exchange. Do not expect collaborative inquiry or mutual recognition; structure group activities around shared challenge and clear, immediate feedback. |
| **It — Exterior-Individual** | Behavior is impulsive, high-intensity, and directly consequence-driven. Skills are acquired rapidly through action, imitation of strong models, and immediate feedback. Long-range behavioral planning is not yet stable. | Immediate feedback loops are essential. Model desired behavior through visibly respected figures. Use short, action-oriented practice sequences. Delayed consequences do not yet function as stable behavioral regulators. |
| **Its — Exterior-Collective** | Social structures are experienced through dominance hierarchies: who is at the top, what they can take or command, and where one fits in the power order. Collective systems are interpreted through personal power logic rather than procedural or structural understanding. | Power dynamics in any group must be addressed explicitly and honestly. Ignoring hierarchy at this stage produces confusion or covert power-seeking. Name the structure; do not pretend authority is absent. |

#### Line Expression at Magic-Mythic/Red

| Line | Characteristic Pattern | What Healthy Expression Looks Like |
|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive** | Early concrete-operational; egocentric perspective; thinking is concrete, action-oriented, and present-tense. Cause-effect is understood when the chain is short and personally felt. Abstract reasoning and coordinated multiple perspectives are not yet stable. | High responsiveness to concrete examples, action-oriented framing, and immediate relevance. Heroic narrative — challenges, victories, and respected exemplars — is the most effective cognitive medium. |
| **Emotional** | Emotions are intense, immediate, and action-generating. The link between felt state and behavior is short; impulse moves rapidly into action. Emotional differentiation is limited: strong-positive and strong-negative predominate over nuanced mid-range states. | Co-regulation through physical activity, rhythm, and short-cycle challenge-reward sequences is primary. Emotional vocabulary training must be embedded in action contexts. Brief reflection is possible only immediately after a strongly felt event. |
| **Moral** | Preconventional Stage 2 (Kohlberg): instrumental exchange — "I'll do what helps me, and I'll respect your deal if you hold your end." No stable internalized moral code beyond immediate consequence and power relation. | Moral learning is best embedded in visible consequence and fair exchange. Abstract moral principles do not yet motivate behavior. Honest acknowledgment of the person's own standards — even if purely self-interested — is more effective than imposing external ethical frameworks prematurely. |
| **Interpersonal** | Relationships are transactional: others are allies, obstacles, or resources. Trust is built through demonstrated strength and reliable exchange. Empathy for others' inner states is not yet load-bearing; perspective-taking is available only when self-interest is served. | Relational engagement works best when framed around respect and mutual benefit. Do not expect perspective-taking as a primary motivational lever. Respect is the primary trust currency — honor it and it will be reciprocated within the stage's logic. |
| **Somatic** | High bodily arousal, vitality, and intensity. The body is the primary site of power and agency. Somatic states are strong signals that move rapidly into action. Slow, long-cycle somatic awareness practices are poorly tolerated. | Short, high-intensity movement and breath practices are well received. Somatic intelligence is strong; redirect it rather than suppress it. Grounding practices that connect bodily intensity to felt consequence (not suppression) are the most effective entry points. |
| **Spiritual/Meaning** | Meaning is generated through heroic action, conquest, and felt power. The sacred is represented by warriors, heroes, and gods of power and victory. Ritual has meaning when it confers or demonstrates strength. | Heroic narrative and embodied challenge are the most accessible meaning-making vehicles. Contemplative or surrender-oriented practices are poorly tolerated at this altitude; strength-honoring and courage-celebrating frames are more resonant. |

#### State Expression at Magic-Mythic/Red

| State Domain | How States Are Typically Accessed and Expressed |
|---|---|
| **Gross/Waking** | High physiological arousal and vitality; high-intensity action states predominate. Focused, deliberate attention is available in short bursts when the task is personally relevant and immediately rewarding. |
| **Mythic/Imaginal** | The imaginal field is populated by heroic figures, enemies, and powerful forces. Peak experiences at this altitude are often felt as direct power encounters — epiphanies of strength, victory, or invincibility — rather than as merged or compassionate states. |
| **Contemplative** | Sustained stillness and open-monitoring practices are poorly tolerated at this altitude. Brief body-based grounding practices (breath, movement, sensation check) are more accessible and appropriate than extended contemplative sessions. |

#### Type Modulation at Magic-Mythic/Red

Types modulate the expression of Red's characteristic patterns without changing the stage logic: an introverted Red may express power through strategic withdrawal and stealth rather than direct confrontation; an extraverted Red will express it loudly and socially. Structure-seeking Reds channel power through hierarchy and clear command; exploratory Reds express it through improvisation and unpredictability. Facilitators should read type modulation carefully to avoid misreading a quiet or strategic power expression as evidence of a different stage.

## Competency-to-Assessment Mapping

| Competency Level | Preferred Assessment Type | Minimum Evidence Requirement | Review Process |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Knowledge check, guided task observation | Completion + accuracy ≥ 70% | Automated or peer-check |
| L2 | Independent performance task, structured reflection | Rubric score ≥ 3/4 + practice log | Peer or facilitator review |
| L3 | Complex scenario application, synthesis product | Rubric score ≥ 4/5 + integration artifact | Facilitator review + blind peer calibration |
| L4 | Teaching or protocol design + trainee/participant outcome data | Rubric ≥ 85% + measurable outcome in others | SME or senior facilitator review |

### Cross-Cutting Assessment Principles

- Use performance-based assessments for L2+ outcomes; knowledge checks alone are insufficient.
- Use retrieval checks (24h, 72h, 7d) for concept durability across all levels.
- Tie rubric criteria explicitly to AQAL dimensions (quadrant, level, line, state, type).
- Grade high-judgment work (L3, L4) with blind-review and at least two independent assessors.
- Flag and review all assessments completed during known dysregulated states.
