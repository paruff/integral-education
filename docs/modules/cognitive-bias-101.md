## 🧠 Learn

**Cognitive bias** = a systematic error in thinking that affects the judgments and decisions people make.

Biases are not signs of stupidity — they are heuristics (mental shortcuts) that evolved to help us act quickly under uncertainty, with limited time and information. They become problems when applied outside their useful domain — when speed is traded for accuracy in situations where accuracy actually matters.

Key biases to know:
- **Confirmation bias** — seeking information that confirms existing beliefs
- **Availability heuristic** — overweighting recent or vivid examples
- **In-group bias** — favoring members of your own group
- **Dunning-Kruger effect** — overestimating competence in areas of limited knowledge — specifically, a metacognitive deficit that prevents low performers from recognizing their own performance gaps. Note: subsequent large-scale replications have found the effect more modest and context-dependent than originally reported
- **Anchoring** — over-relying on the first piece of information encountered

## Two Framework Lenses: Heuristics-and-Biases, and the Metacognitive Model

This list of five biases draws on two related but distinct research programs. Kahneman and Tversky's heuristics-and-biases program (the source of confirmation bias, availability heuristic, and anchoring) treats bias as a predictable byproduct of fast, associative ("System 1") thinking substituting for slower, effortful ("System 2") reasoning — the shortcuts are efficient most of the time and systematically wrong in specific, identifiable conditions. The Dunning-Kruger metacognitive model is a narrower, different claim: it is specifically about the accuracy of *self-assessment* — whether you can correctly judge your own competence — rather than about errors in judging the external world.

These two lenses layer usefully: the heuristics-and-biases frame explains why a judgment about the *world* goes wrong, while the metacognitive frame explains why a judgment about *your own reasoning* can also go wrong, including your ability to detect the first kind of error in yourself. Treating all five biases as one undifferentiated category obscures this distinction — and the metacognitive layer is exactly why practicing bias-detection on your own thinking is harder than spotting it in someone else's.

## The Limitations of the Heuristics-and-Biases Framework

The heuristics-and-biases framework is well-established but has real limits worth naming honestly.

First, the replication record is mixed. Some biases (anchoring, confirmation bias) replicate robustly across contexts; others, including the original Dunning-Kruger effect size, have proven more modest and more context-dependent under large-scale replication than early popular accounts suggested. Treating any single bias-effect size as fixed and universal overstates what the research supports.

Second, labeling a judgment "biased" after the fact is easy and can itself become a rhetorical shortcut — a way of dismissing a conclusion you dislike without engaging its actual content. Bias-language is diagnostic of a *process*, not automatically proof that a specific *conclusion* is wrong; a biased process can still, on occasion, produce a correct answer, and an unbiased process can still err for other reasons.

Third, heuristics are heuristics because they usually work. A framework that only catalogs failure modes can leave the impression that fast, intuitive judgment is inherently suspect — when in fact most everyday decisions do not have the stakes or the available time to justify slow, effortful System 2 processing. The skill this module builds is knowing *when* the shortcut is likely to mislead, not abandoning shortcuts altogether.

## 🧭 Orient

You are about to apply this bias list to a real decision or opinion of your own, not a textbook example. Before starting, notice that spotting a bias in someone else's reasoning is easy and spotting it in your own is the actual point of this practice — the discomfort of the second is a feature, not an obstacle.

## 🌿 Encounter

Bring to mind a recent moment when you felt unusually certain about something — a decision, a judgment about a person, a prediction about how something would turn out. Notice what that certainty felt like from the inside: solid, obvious, not requiring further checking. That felt-obviousness is worth remembering, because it is exactly the signal that a heuristic may be doing the work rather than a fully examined chain of reasoning — which is what the practice below asks you to check.

## 🧘 Practice

**Duration:** 10–15 min

**Instructions:**
1. Recall a recent decision or strong opinion you hold
2. Ask: *What evidence supports this?*
3. Ask: *What evidence contradicts this?*
4. Identify which bias may be operating (use the list above)
5. Write one sentence that steelmans the opposing view

---

## ⚓ Stabilize

## Checkpoint: Genuine Bias-Detection, Performative Doubt, and Bias-Shopping

After completing the practice, distinguish three patterns that can each look like bias-awareness but are not equivalent.

**Genuine bias-detection** looks like: you named a specific bias that was actually operating, found at least one piece of disconfirming evidence you had genuinely overlooked, and the steelmanned opposing view changed your confidence level at least slightly.

**Performative doubt** looks similar but is not the same — saying "I might be biased here" as a verbal hedge without actually identifying which bias, what evidence was ignored, or updating anything. The language of humility is present without the underlying cognitive work.

**Bias-shopping** is the subtler failure mode: reaching for whichever named bias best excuses a conclusion you already hold, rather than the one that actually best describes what happened in your reasoning. This can look identical to genuine self-analysis while functioning as a new, more sophisticated way of avoiding it.

If your Practice write-up names a bias but your confidence in the original belief did not move at all, that is useful information — return to step 3 (contradicting evidence) and check whether it was genuinely weighed.

## The Gifts of Bias Awareness

Learning to recognize your own cognitive biases offers something specific: the ability to hold a strong feeling of certainty and a genuine question ("but is this actually right?") at the same time, without one collapsing into the other. That combination — conviction plus checkability — is more useful in practice than either pure confidence or pure doubt.

It also changes how disagreement feels. When you can name the specific heuristic that might be shaping your view, someone else's different conclusion stops reading as a threat and starts reading as a data point — possibly they are seeing a bias in your reasoning that you cannot see from the inside, which is exactly what an outside perspective is good for.

Finally, this skill transfers everywhere decisions get made under uncertainty and time pressure — which is nearly everywhere. The habit of pausing to ask "what shortcut might be operating here?" before a consequential decision is a small, repeatable action with outsized returns over a lifetime of decisions.

## 🔄 Integrate

Apply this in real life:
- Pause before forming conclusions and ask: *Am I being systematic?*
- Seek out one piece of disconfirming evidence before deciding
- Practice steelmanning: state the strongest version of an opposing view
- **Bridge to Critical Thinking Foundations.** This module builds *reactive* skill — catching a bias already present. [Critical Thinking Foundations](./critical-thinking-foundations) builds *proactive* skill — constructing arguments that are sound from the start. Use both together.
- **Bridge to Evidence Evaluation.** [Evidence Evaluation](./evidence-evaluation) turns the same self-questioning instinct outward, toward the quality of evidence in sources you encounter rather than the shortcuts in your own reasoning.

<Admonition type="tip" title="Facilitator Note">

- Encourage participants to choose a real, current decision or opinion for the Practice exercise rather than a hypothetical one — bias is much easier to spot in the abstract than in one's own live reasoning.
- Watch for the Stabilize checkpoint's "bias-shopping" pattern in group discussion — a participant naming a bias that conveniently excuses their conclusion rather than the one that actually fits.
- The Dunning-Kruger effect is frequently overstated in popular reference; note the replication caveat explicitly if it comes up, to avoid reinforcing an inflated version of the finding.
- This is an entry-level module for many participants — keep the tone curious and low-stakes rather than diagnostic ("you're biased") to avoid triggering defensiveness that shuts down the practice.

## 📚 Evidence and Citations

Evidence quality is rated as Tier A (primary research or strong empirical base), Tier B (theoretical synthesis or clinical-practitioner evidence), or Tier C (provisional/expert opinion). See the [Evidence Vetting Checklist](/internal/quality/evidence-vetting-checklist) for the full tier definitions.

| Claim | Source | Quality | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heuristics-and-biases program: fast, associative judgment ("System 1") systematically diverges from slower, effortful reasoning ("System 2") in identifiable conditions, producing predictable errors including anchoring and availability effects | Kahneman, D. *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), Farrar, Straus and Giroux; building on Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," *Science*, 185(4157), 1124–1131 (1974) | Tier A — synthesis of decades of experimental research; foundational and extensively replicated across many of its core findings | Some specific effect sizes have not replicated as strongly as originally reported; the broad dual-process framing is influential but simplifies a more complex and debated cognitive architecture |
| Confirmation bias: the tendency to search for, interpret, and recall information in ways that confirm prior beliefs | Nickerson, R. S. "Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many Guises," *Review of General Psychology*, 2(2), 175–220 (1998) | Tier A — comprehensive review synthesizing decades of experimental literature | Effect magnitude varies by domain and motivation; confirmation bias is robust but not universal in every reasoning context |
| Anchoring effect: initial reference points, even arbitrary ones, systematically bias subsequent numerical and evaluative judgments | Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," *Science*, 185(4157), 1124–1131 (1974) | Tier A — original experimental demonstration; extensively replicated across domains including negotiation, pricing, and legal judgments | Effect size varies with expertise and motivation to deliberate; experts are not immune but can be somewhat less susceptible in their domain of expertise |
| Dunning-Kruger effect: low performers in a skill domain tend to overestimate their competence due to the same lack of skill that produces poor performance in the first place | Kruger, J., & Dunning, D. "Unskilled and Unaware of It," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 77(6), 1121–1134 (1999) | Tier B — original studies are widely cited, but subsequent large-scale replications and statistical re-analyses (e.g., regression-to-the-mean critiques) suggest the effect is smaller and more context-dependent than the original framing implied | Popular accounts substantially overstate the size and universality of this effect; treat as a real but modest and debated pattern, not a fixed law of self-assessment |
| In-group bias: preferential treatment and trust extended to members of one's own group, even when group membership is arbitrary or minimal | Tajfel, H., & Turner, J. C. "An Integrative Theory of Intergroup Conflict," in *The Social Psychology of Intergroup Relations* (1979), Brooks/Cole | Tier A — foundational social psychology research (minimal group paradigm), extensively replicated across cultures and contexts | Effect size and expression vary substantially by cultural context and the salience of competing group identities |
