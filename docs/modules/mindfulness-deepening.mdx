## 🧠 Learn

## The Progression: Three Modes of Meditative Attention

Contemporary contemplative neuroscience distinguishes two complementary forms of attention in meditation (Lutz et al., 2008, *PNAS*):

| Mode | Also called | Key quality |
|---|---|---|
| **Focused Attention (FA)** | Shamatha, samatha, concentration | Repeatedly directing attention to a single object; detecting and recovering from distraction |
| **Open Monitoring (OM)** | Vipassanā, choiceless awareness, mindfulness of mindfulness | Attending to the field of experience as a whole without selecting any particular object |

This module adds a third mode that emerges from deepened OM practice:

| Mode | Also called | Key quality |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-referential Awareness (NRA)** | Rigpa (Dzogchen), witnessing, pure presence | Awareness that does not require an object; the knowing quality rests in itself |

These three modes are not steps you climb and abandon. They form a living range. Advanced practitioners move fluidly among them within a single session.

## Focused Attention: Deepening Concentration

Basic mindfulness stabilises attention on the breath. The next move is to **deepen** that stability until the quality of concentration changes. In the Theravada Buddhist tradition, access concentration (*upacāra samādhi*) precedes absorption (*appanā samādhi*) and the jhāna states (Brasington, *Right Concentration*, 2015).

Signs of concentration deepening:

- **Spontaneous stillness** — the need to redirect attention decreases dramatically.
- **Absorption interest** — the meditation object becomes intrinsically engaging, not effortfully held.
- ***Pīti* and *sukha*** — a quality of pleasurable interest or subtle joy arising in the body-mind.
- **Nimitta** — a bright, stable inner sign (visual, felt, or auditory) indicating entry into access concentration.

These are natural consequences of sustained practice, not goals to fabricate. Grasping for jhāna or forcing a nimitta will collapse the concentration.

## Open Monitoring: Choiceless Awareness

Once attention is reasonably stable, you can release the specific object (breath) and open to the **whole field of present-moment experience** without selecting any particular element. This is what Krishnamurti called "choiceless awareness" and what later researchers coded as Open Monitoring (Lutz et al., 2008).

In OM practice:

- Nothing is excluded; nothing is highlighted.
- Sounds, body sensations, thoughts, emotions, and perceptions arise and pass without the meditator chasing or suppressing any of them.
- The practitioner develops sensitivity to **the arising and passing of experience itself** — the direct experiential base for insight (*vipassanā*).

OM practice begins to reveal the three characteristics (*tilakkhaṇa*) of experience: impermanence (*anicca*), the absence of a fixed suffering-producing self (*anattā*), and the unsatisfactoriness of clinging to what passes (*dukkha*).

## Noting: Deconstructing the Self-Process

Noting is a technique formalised in the Mahasi Sayadaw tradition (*Manual of Insight*, trans. Bhikkhu Anālayo, 2016) and made accessible to contemporary practitioners by teachers such as Kenneth Folk (*Contemplative Fitness*, 2013) and Daniel Ingram (*Mastering the Core Teachings of the Buddha*, 2008).

**What noting is:** A meditator applies a quiet mental label to each arising phenomenon — *hearing*, *thinking*, *planning*, *warmth*, *pressure*, *pleasant*, *unpleasant*. The label is not analysis; it is a light, rapid touch that keeps the meditator from *becoming* the experience.

**Why it deconstructs the self-process:** The sense of a unified, continuous self is partly constructed by fast, automatic identification with arising phenomena ("I am anxious," "I am the thinker of this thought"). Noting inserts a micro-gap between experience and identification. Repeated consistently, this gap reveals:

- No two "selves" are the same from moment to moment.
- What feels like a unified "I" is actually a rapid sequence of transient mental events.
- The process of *constructing* selfhood becomes visible — the direct experiential correlate of *anattā* (non-self) as understood in the Mahasi tradition.

This is not destabilising nihilism. Practiced carefully, it is a relief — the loosening of unnecessarily tight identification with every thought, sensation, and emotion.

## From Noting to Non-Referential Awareness

As noting practice matures, the labels become lighter and less necessary. What remains is a luminous, effortless recognition of each arising — without the machinery of labelling. This is the edge of **non-referential awareness**: awareness that knows without requiring an object or a "knower."

In Dzogchen and Tibetan Mahamudra traditions, this quality is called *rigpa* — the naked, self-knowing nature of awareness. In Wilber's AQAL framing, it corresponds to early causal-state access and the stabilisation of the witness perspective (Wilber, *Integral Spirituality*, 2006).

The [Causal / Witness State](./causal-witness-state) module addresses this territory in depth. This module prepares the ground.

## Two Framework Lenses: Contemplative Neuroscience and Classical Meditation Traditions

This module draws on two complementary lenses. Lutz, Slagter, and Davidson's contemplative neuroscience research supplies the empirical, cross-tradition vocabulary — Focused Attention and Open Monitoring as measurable, distinguishable attentional modes with distinct neural signatures. The classical meditation traditions (Theravada jhāna instruction, Mahasi-style noting, Dzogchen framing of *rigpa*) supply the detailed, centuries-refined practice instructions that the neuroscience vocabulary describes at a more abstract level.

Used together, these lenses ground traditional practice instructions in contemporary, testable categories while preserving the experiential precision that only long lineages of practitioners have developed. Neuroscience alone would tell you attention is changing without telling you how to change it; the traditions alone would give you rich instruction without a shared vocabulary for comparing what different practitioners are actually experiencing.

## The Limitations of These Concentration and Insight Frameworks

These frameworks have real limits worth naming honestly. First, the neuroscience research (Lutz et al., Jha et al.) has generally studied experienced meditators or MBSR-style short interventions — it does not fully validate the specific, escalating practices in this module, which draw more heavily on traditional instruction than on directly replicated experimental protocols.

Second, terms like *pīti*, *nimitta*, and *rigpa* come from specific contemplative lineages and are not universal, culture-neutral descriptions of a fixed underlying experience. Learners should hold this vocabulary as a useful pointer borrowed from a particular tradition, not as a definitive scientific taxonomy of what is happening.

Third, as the Safety Note below emphasizes, the self-deconstructing quality of noting and non-referential awareness practice is genuinely destabilising for some learners, particularly those with a trauma history or fragile self-structure. These are not universally benign relaxation techniques — they are targeted at loosening identification with the self-process, and that loosening carries real risk alongside its real benefit.

## 🧭 Orient

This module asks you to deliberately loosen structures — concentrated focus, fixed self-identification — that ordinary functioning depends on. Before beginning, understand that some disorientation during practice is expected and workable; each practice below includes an explicit grounding return path for exactly this reason. You are not expected to arrive at non-referential awareness on a fixed schedule.

## 🌿 Encounter

Recall, if you can, a moment from ordinary daily life when your attention was unusually stable and effortless — reading, listening to music, or simply resting quietly — even without formal meditation training. That stability, however brief, is the same quality this module deliberately extends and deepens. You are building on a capacity you already have some access to, not manufacturing something foreign.

## 🧘 Practice

## Practice 1 — Concentration Escalation Protocol (4-Stage Sitting)

**Purpose:** Systematically deepen focused-attention concentration beyond basic breath awareness.

**Duration:** 30–45 minutes

### Stage 1 — Gross Breath Anchor (5–10 minutes)

1. Sit with spine upright, eyes closed or softly cast down.
2. Choose a specific, stable anchor point for the breath — the tip of the nostrils, the upper lip, or the rising and falling of the belly.
3. Hold attention on the physical sensation of breath at that one location.
4. When attention drifts, note *wandering* or *thinking* briefly, and return. Recovery is the practice.
5. Aim for continuous contact with the sensation for at least 10 consecutive breaths before moving on.

### Stage 2 — Narrowing (5–10 minutes)

6. Narrow the field of attention still further — to the briefest moment of sensation at the inhalation's entry point. Exclude the rest of the breath cycle.
7. The object becomes smaller and more refined. This increases concentration density.
8. If the object seems to disappear, stay at the location — subtle sensation is still present.

### Stage 3 — Absorptive Invitation (10–15 minutes)

9. Release any deliberate effort to "do" the narrowing. Hold the location lightly.
10. If a quality of warmth, interest, or subtle pleasure (*pīti/sukha*) arises, gently acknowledge it and allow it to deepen.
11. If a bright inner sign (nimitta) appears — a light, shape, or energetic impression — acknowledge it without grasping. Let it stabilise naturally.
12. You may notice a significant reduction in the felt need to redirect attention. This is access concentration. Rest here.

### Stage 4 — Open Expansion (5–10 minutes)

13. From the concentrated baseline, allow the field of attention to expand gradually — first to the whole body, then to sounds, then to the whole present-moment field.
14. The quality of concentrated stillness should carry forward into the expanded field.
15. Close the session with three slow breaths and a brief body scan to return fully to ordinary awareness.

**Frequency:** 3–5 sessions per week.

## Practice 2 — Choiceless Awareness (Open Monitoring)

**Purpose:** Develop the capacity to rest in the whole field of present-moment experience without selecting or suppressing any element.

**Duration:** 20–30 minutes (can follow Practice 1 Stage 4 directly)

**Script (read slowly, or record and play back):**

*Settle into your posture. Take three grounding breaths.*

*Allow the breath to be present — but release it as a dedicated object. You are no longer following the breath. You are now opening to everything that is arising.*

*Let sounds come and go. You do not need to name them. Simply let them arise and pass through the field of your awareness.*

*Let sensations in the body arise — pressure, warmth, movement, tingling. Let them be present without needing to adjust them.*

*Let thoughts arise. They are not a problem. They arise in awareness like sounds — they come, they pass. You do not need to follow them or push them away.*

*Let emotions or moods be present. Whatever tone is present in the field — neutral, pleasant, uncomfortable — let it exist without urgency.*

*Your task is simply this: to remain present to the whole field without preference. Nothing needs to be different from what it is right now.*

*Rest here for fifteen minutes. If you find yourself in a train of thought, simply notice that noticing is already happening — and return to the open field.*

*To close: take three slow breaths. Notice your body. Open your eyes.*

**Anchor note:** If you feel lost or overwhelmed at any point, return to the breath for 60 seconds, then re-open. The breath is always available as a grounding anchor.

## Practice 3 — Noting for Self-Process Deconstruction

**Purpose:** Use rapid, precise mental labelling to interrupt automatic identification and observe the self-construction process directly.

**Duration:** 15–25 minutes

**Instructions:**

1. Sit comfortably. Begin with three grounding breaths.
2. Set a gentle intention: *I am going to notice what arises and name it lightly.*
3. Begin noting at a rhythm of roughly once per second — fast enough to keep the labelling from becoming analysis. Use simple categories:
   - Physical: *pressure*, *warmth*, *tension*, *movement*, *pain*, *pleasure*
   - Auditory: *hearing*, *sound*
   - Visual (inner): *seeing*, *image*
   - Mental: *thinking*, *planning*, *remembering*, *judging*, *imagining*
   - Emotional: *pleasant*, *unpleasant*, *neutral*, *anxious*, *content*
   - Reactive: *wanting*, *resisting*, *comparing*
4. The label is a quiet mental word — not spoken aloud, not an analysis.
5. When you catch yourself in a long chain of thought without noting, notice *thinking* — and return.
6. After 10–15 minutes: slow the noting. Allow gaps. Notice what is present between notes.
7. In the last few minutes: ask silently — *Who is doing the noting?* Do not answer conceptually. Simply attend to the question as a felt sensation and observe what arises.

**Grounding check:** If you feel unmoored, unsettled, or disconnected at any point, press your feet into the floor, open your eyes, and take five slow breaths. The practice is meant to loosen identification, not produce anxiety.

## 🔍 Reflect

Record your responses in a journal after each session. Honesty, not spiritual attainment, is the measure here.

1. During concentration practice (Practice 1): Was there any session where the quality of concentration felt qualitatively different — more absorbed, less effortful? If so, what preceded that shift?
2. During choiceless awareness (Practice 2): What was the most challenging element to let pass without grasping or suppressing? What does that pattern reveal about your habitual attention?
3. During noting (Practice 3): Were there moments when the label appeared to arise *before* the reaction it named — as if awareness was faster than identification? What was that like?
4. The practices introduce the idea that the "self" is partly a construction. What is your felt response to that — resistance, curiosity, relief, confusion? How does that response itself arise in awareness?
5. Do you notice any changes in how you experience ordinary daily life — conversations, transitions, emotional reactions — after consistent practice with this module?

## 📊 Assess

## 5-Point Self-Rating Scales

Rate yourself on the following scales after each formal sitting session. Tracking your ratings over time reveals patterns more accurately than any single data point.

### Concentration Depth

| Rating | Description |
|---|---|
| **1 — Scattered** | Attention is predominantly in thought-trains. Little sustained contact with the breath or body. Repeated drift without recovery. |
| **2 — Intermittent** | Some sustained attention is possible (5–10 consecutive breaths), but drift is frequent and recovery takes noticeable effort. |
| **3 — Stable** | Sustained contact for 15–20 consecutive breaths is accessible. Drift occurs but recovery is quick and natural. |
| **4 — Concentrated** | Significant reduction in the need to redirect attention. The object is intrinsically engaging. A quality of interest, stillness, or subtle pleasure (*pīti*) may be present. |
| **5 — Absorbed** | Access concentration: the object is vivid and bright; attention rests naturally without effort; distracting thoughts become very quiet or absent; nimitta (inner luminosity or sign) may appear. |

### Awareness Stability

| Rating | Description |
|---|---|
| **1 — Reactive** | Experiences arise and immediately pull attention into identification or reaction. Little or no meta-awareness present. |
| **2 — Noticing (delayed)** | Awareness of the arising of experience is present, but typically retrospective — noticed after identification, not during. |
| **3 — Present-moment noticing** | Can notice experiences as they arise with moderate immediacy. Open monitoring is possible for short periods (3–5 minutes). |
| **4 — Sustained open field** | Can rest in the open field (choiceless awareness) for 10+ minutes without significant collapse into object-fixation or reactive thought-trains. |
| **5 — Non-referential glimpses** | Periods in which awareness rests in itself without requiring an object. The noting process may simplify or become transparent. The question *Who is aware?* opens rather than closes. |

**Progress target:** Reach ratings of 3 or above on both scales in the majority of sessions over a two-week period before proceeding to the [Causal / Witness State](./causal-witness-state) module.

## The Gifts of Mindfulness Deepening

This module offers a genuine bridge between two kinds of practice that often stay unconnected: the concentrated stability of Focused Attention and the more expansive availability of Open Monitoring. Learners who cultivate both often find each supports the other — concentration provides the stability that makes open awareness workable rather than diffuse, and open awareness prevents concentration from becoming a rigid, effortful fixation.

It also offers something rarer: a direct, repeatable, experiential encounter with the constructed nature of the self-sense, through noting practice. This is not an abstract philosophical claim taken on faith — it is something the practices in this module let a learner observe directly, at their own pace, with explicit grounding support available throughout.

Finally, the three-mode framework (FA, OM, NRA) gives learners a shared vocabulary for describing subtle shifts in their own practice — useful both for self-tracking via the Assess rating scales and for communicating with a teacher or facilitator about what is actually happening in a given sitting.

## Stabilize

**The Pre/Trans Distinction (Inflation Warning):** Glimpses of non-referential awareness or the loosening of self-identification during noting practice can feel like arrival at a permanent, elevated state. As the Limitations and Safety Note sections make clear, these are genuine but temporary state openings — not enduring stage achievements. Continue treating them as territory to integrate, not credentials to claim.

**Consolidation, not achievement:** The Assess section's rating scales exist to track gradual, session-by-session capacity rather than to mark a single decisive breakthrough. Expect the ratings to fluctuate — a strong session followed by a scattered one is normal and does not indicate lost progress.

## 🔄 Integrate

Carry the skills from this module into daily-life contexts:

- **Before difficult conversations:** Take 60 seconds of choiceless awareness — open to the whole present-moment field — before entering an emotionally charged exchange. Notice whether it changes the quality of your listening.
- **In moments of strong emotion:** Apply a gentle note — *anger*, *fear*, *longing*, *frustration*. Observe whether the note creates a micro-gap between the emotion's energy and automatic identification with it.
- **During repetitive tasks:** While walking, washing up, or commuting, sustain gentle noting for 5–10 minutes. This extends practice off the cushion.
- **As a transition ritual:** At the end of the workday or before sleep, sit for 10 minutes of Practice 2 (Choiceless Awareness) as a deliberate transition from doing-mode to being-mode.

**Natural next step:** When concentration depth and awareness stability are consistently at 3–4 on both scales, you are ready for the [Causal / Witness State](./causal-witness-state) module, which introduces formless open-awareness, self-inquiry, and stable witnessing.

:::caution Safety Note

This module is rated **Tier 2** for individuals with a personal history of dissociation, depersonalisation, derealisation, trauma, psychosis-spectrum experience, or significant identity instability. For practitioners with a stable foundation and no relevant history, it operates as a **Tier 1 (self-guided)** module.

**Consent:** Proceed when you are willing to observe your own mental activity — including self-referential thoughts — without necessarily acting on what arises. You may pause or stop any practice at any time. All three practices include explicit grounding return paths.

**Contraindications:** If you are currently experiencing active dissociation, psychosis, mania, severe depression, or a trauma response, do not undertake this module without guidance from a qualified mental health professional or trained contemplative facilitator. This platform does not provide clinical care.

**Stop rules:** If noting or open monitoring practice produces significant anxiety, depersonalisation, or a distressing sense of self-fragmentation, stop immediately. Open your eyes. Press both feet firmly into the floor. Name five objects you can see. Take five slow breaths. If disorientation persists beyond five minutes, contact a qualified support person.

**Grounding alternative:** If concentration practices produce tension, strain, or forced effort, shift to the simpler breath-anchor from [Mindfulness Basics](./mindfulness-basics) until ease returns, then re-enter gradually.

**Spiritual bypassing caution:** Insight into non-self (*anattā*) is a cognitive-experiential event — a shift in how you understand and directly perceive yourself — it does not dissolve developmental needs in emotional, relational, or shadow lines. Continue shadow, emotional, and relational work alongside state practice.

**Escalation:** If any distress or disorientation persists, step away from the practice and contact a trusted support person or qualified practitioner.

For the full safety framework, see the [State Development Safety Standard](/internal/safety/state-development-safety-standard). If intense altered-state experiences arise unexpectedly, consult the [Spiritual Bypassing Red Flags](/internal/safety/shadowwork-safety-standard#spiritual-bypassing-red-flags).

:::
