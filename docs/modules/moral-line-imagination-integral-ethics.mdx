# 🧭 Overview

Beyond Kohlberg's Stage 6, the mature moral life is not dominated by a single framework — not pure duty, not pure consequence, not pure virtue. Integral ethics, as described by Ken Wilber and grounded in Mark Johnson's concept of **moral imagination**, is the capacity to use multiple moral frameworks simultaneously as *lenses* rather than as competing final truths.

Moral imagination (Johnson, 1993) is the cognitive-affective capacity to imaginatively inhabit a moral situation from multiple perspectives at once — to feel the competing moral pulls without prematurely resolving them, to hold the tension as productive rather than paralyzing, and ultimately to act from a place of integrated conviction. This requires postformal cognitive operations (the ability to hold multiple systems in dynamic tension) and a stable, cultivated character as the foundation that makes principled action reliable rather than merely occasional.

**Virtue ethics** (Aristotle, MacIntyre, Narvaez) is the essential developmental complement to Kohlberg's structural and Gilligan's relational approaches. Character — stable dispositional orientations toward good action — is not an add-on to moral development; it is the substrate that makes moral knowledge actionable. Practical wisdom (*phronesis*) is the virtue that integrates all the others: the cultivated capacity for context-sensitive, calibrated moral judgment that cannot be reduced to rule-following.

# 🧭 Orient

## Four Moral Frameworks as Complementary Lenses

Integral ethics does not require choosing between moral frameworks. It requires learning to *use* them as complementary lenses — each illuminating a dimension of the moral situation the others underweight. The four frameworks below are presented not as a ranked list of developmental stages, but as a structured repertoire of moral perspectives available to the mature moral reasoner.

### Lens 1 — Deontological Ethics (Kant → Kohlberg Stage 6)

**Core question:** *What are my duties and the rights of all persons in this situation?*

Deontological ethics grounds moral action in **duty and rights** rather than in consequences. Immanuel Kant's formulation — act only on the principle you would will to become universal law — provides the deontological foundation. Kohlberg's Stage 6 operationalizes this as the universal ethical principles (human dignity, equal worth, justice independent of social approval) that take precedence over law, convention, and majority opinion when they conflict.

**What this lens illuminates:** Constraints on action that cannot be overridden by good outcomes. The person who may not be sacrificed for the collective good. The duty that remains binding even when violating it would produce better consequences. Rights as side-constraints on otherwise permissible acts.

**What this lens underweights:** Context, relationship, and the cumulative consequences of rigid rule-following in complex situations. The lived texture of moral particularity.

**Developmental note:** Stage 6 reasoning is the structural apex of Kohlberg's sequence — not a frame for all moral reasoning, but the principled foundation to which other frameworks must answer when they tempt us toward convenient exceptions.

---

### Lens 2 — Consequentialist Ethics (Mill → Postconventional Outcomes Thinking)

**Core question:** *What action produces the best outcomes for all affected parties?*

Consequentialist ethics — most fully articulated by John Stuart Mill's utilitarianism — grounds moral action in **outcomes and welfare**: the greatest good for the greatest number. Postconventional consequentialism does not reduce this to a simple calculation; it requires rigorously identifying all affected parties, weighing short- and long-term effects, and attending to distributional questions (who bears the costs? who receives the benefits?) that simple majority-welfare calculations obscure.

**What this lens illuminates:** The real-world consequences of rule-following when rules produce systematic harm. Trade-offs among competing goods. The moral weight of scale — the difference between a harm to one and a harm to millions.

**What this lens underweights:** Inviolable duties and rights. The person who is harmed so that the many may benefit. The moral significance of *how* good outcomes are achieved, not only that they are achieved.

**Developmental note:** Postconventional consequentialist thinking requires the cognitive capacity to model complex systems, anticipate second-order effects, and hold distributional fairness alongside aggregate welfare. This is a demanding epistemic task; premature certainty about outcomes is a common failure mode.

---

### Lens 3 — Virtue Ethics (Aristotle → MacIntyre → Narvaez)

**Core question:** *What would a person of excellent character do in this situation? What virtues is this situation calling forth in me?*

Virtue ethics grounds moral action not in rules or consequences but in **character** — the stable dispositional orientations toward excellent action that are cultivated through practice, community, and moral formation over time. Aristotle's foundational text (*Nicomachean Ethics*) identifies the virtues as stable character traits (courage, justice, temperance, practical wisdom) that constitute human flourishing (*eudaimonia*). Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981) revives and extends this tradition as a response to the failure of modern moral philosophy to ground ethical action without a substantive account of the good. Darcia Narvaez's neuroscientific research (*Neurobiology and the Development of Human Morality*, 2014) provides empirical grounding for the claim that character — moral habituation beginning in early development — is the substrate on which all higher moral reasoning depends.

**Phronesis — practical wisdom:** The central virtue in the tradition is *phronesis*, practical wisdom: the cultivated capacity for context-sensitive moral judgment that cannot be reduced to the application of rules. The phronimos (person of practical wisdom) perceives the morally salient features of a particular situation clearly, identifies the relevant principles and precedents, and acts with calibrated, proportionate responsiveness — neither rigidly following rules when context demands flexibility, nor abandoning principles when self-interest makes flexibility convenient.

**What this lens illuminates:** The character of the agent as a moral determinant, not only the act or its consequences. The gap between knowing what is right and reliably doing it — what Narvaez and Rest called the moral action gap. The role of moral habituation, community, and practice in building genuine moral reliability.

**What this lens underweights:** Universal rights and duties that apply regardless of agent character. Systematic analysis of consequences at scale. The structural injustices that character-focused ethics can fail to address.

**Developmental note:** Virtue ethics is not a regression to pre-rational moral intuition. It is a sophisticated recognition that character precedes and enables principled moral action — that rules without character produce compliance, not virtue.

---

### Lens 4 — Care Ethics (Gilligan → Noddings)

**Core question:** *What response genuinely honors my responsibility to this particular person in this relationship? Who depends on me, and what does that dependence require?*

Care ethics, developed by Carol Gilligan in response to Kohlberg's abstraction of moral reasoning from relationship, grounds moral action in **relational responsibility and compassionate attentiveness**. The care orientation begins with particularity — this person, this relationship, this moment of need — rather than with universal principle. Nel Noddings extended Gilligan's foundational insight into a full care ethics theory grounded in natural caring (the lived experience of care between parent and child) and ethical caring (the extension of care orientation to strangers through cultivated responsiveness).

**What this lens illuminates:** The moral weight of particular relationships and dependencies. The harm caused by treating persons as abstract bearers of rights rather than as particular, irreplaceable individuals embedded in webs of relationship. The care required to maintain relationships of trust, vulnerability, and mutual responsiveness.

**What this lens underweights:** Universal duties that apply beyond the boundaries of existing relationships. The potential parochialism of care: favoring those to whom we are already related at the expense of those to whom we are not.

**Developmental note:** Care Position 3 (Gilligan's integrated care, developed in the previous module) is the foundation for integral care ethics: genuine responsibility for self and other without the self-erasure of Position 2. Care ethics at the integral level includes rather than excludes universal principles — it holds the universal and the particular in productive tension.

## Moral Imagination as the Integrative Capacity

Mark Johnson (*Moral Imagination*, 1993) argues that genuine moral reasoning is fundamentally imaginative — it requires the capacity to *inhabit* a moral situation from multiple perspectives simultaneously, to feel the competing moral claims as genuinely compelling rather than dismissing some in favor of others, and to move toward action without premature closure.

Moral imagination is not the same as moral indecision. It is the capacity to:

1. **Map the moral landscape** — identify which frameworks are illuminating which dimensions of the situation, and where they converge versus where they genuinely diverge.
2. **Hold the tension** — feel the competing moral pulls without either collapsing them into a premature consensus or becoming paralyzed by their divergence.
3. **Act from integrated conviction** — make a decision that honors the full complexity of the situation, accepts the trade-offs required, and is grounded in character rather than only in abstract principle.

**The cognitive operations required:** Holding four frameworks simultaneously as lenses — rather than applying them sequentially or choosing among them — requires postformal cognitive operations, specifically what Commons and Richards call metasystematic reasoning: the ability to operate *across* multiple formal systems at once rather than within any single one. This is the cognitive correlate of what Wilber identifies as the Teal-to-Turquoise transition in integral stage theory.

<Admonition type="info" title="Why this module matters">

Integral ethics is not moral relativism. It is not the position that all frameworks are equally valid in every situation or that no moral judgment is possible. It is the capacity to use multiple frameworks as rigorous analytical tools — and to act with principled conviction even when the frameworks diverge. The moral imagination that makes this possible is a developable capacity, not a natural gift.

</Admonition>

## (a) Four-Lens Moral Analysis

Identify **one real moral situation you are currently facing** — a genuine dilemma in your life, work, or relationships that carries real moral weight and has not yet been resolved. It should be a situation in which you feel genuine uncertainty, competing obligations, or a pull between different goods.

Analyze the situation through all four frameworks. Complete the full table before reading across the frameworks:

| Framework | Core question this framework asks | What this framework says you should do and why | What this framework underweights in this situation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deontological** | What duties and rights are at stake? | | |
| **Consequentialist** | What action produces the best outcomes for all affected? | | |
| **Virtue ethics** | What would a person of excellent character do? | | |
| **Care ethics** | What does genuine relational responsibility require? | | |

After completing the table:

1. **Map convergences:** Which frameworks agree? On what? What does their convergence signal about what action is clearly required?
2. **Map divergences:** Where do the frameworks genuinely conflict? These are the **productive moral tensions** — the places where the situation's genuine moral complexity lives. Do not rush to resolve them.
3. **Write 5–8 sentences:** What do the divergences reveal about this situation that you had not seen before entering the analysis? What moral weight does each framework add that the others miss?

## (c) Phronesis Practice

Phronesis — practical wisdom — is the virtue that integrates the others into context-sensitive moral action. It cannot be learned from rules alone; it is developed through deliberate attention to recurring moral situations that require calibrated judgment.

**Identify one recurring situation in your life that requires moral judgment** — a situation that arises regularly, that you cannot resolve by applying a simple rule, and in which the appropriate response requires reading context, weighing competing considerations, and acting with proportionate, responsive care.

Complete the mapping:

# 🧲 Stabilize

## Pre/Trans checkpoint: genuine moral imagination vs. common failure modes

<Admonition type="warning" title="Critical distinction">

The language and posture of integral ethics can be adopted intellectually without the character development that makes it genuine. The checkpoint below is a structural diagnostic, not a character judgment. Apply it rigorously to your own reasoning.

</Admonition>

### Distinction A: Genuine moral imagination vs. moral eclecticism

**Genuine moral imagination:** You hold all four frameworks as genuinely binding lenses. When a framework illuminates a moral demand you would prefer not to face — a duty you would rather not honor, a consequence you have been avoiding seeing, a virtue you have not cultivated — you do not dismiss that framework as "less relevant here." The frameworks constrain as well as illuminate.

The reasoning process feels: **humble** (complexity is real), **complex** (there are genuine tensions), **action-oriented** (you are moving toward a decision, not away from one).

**Moral eclecticism:** You select the framework that validates the position you already hold and dismiss the others as inappropriate, overly abstract, culturally biased, or otherwise inapplicable to *this* particular situation. The frameworks function as rationalizations, not as constraints. The outcome of any multi-framework analysis is predictably favorable to your preferred position.

**Diagnostic question:** *Does applying all four frameworks constrain any of my options, or do they all converge conveniently on what I wanted to do anyway?*

---

### Distinction B: Genuine moral imagination vs. paralysis by moral complexity

**Genuine moral imagination:** You hold the tension of competing frameworks *in order to act better* — not to avoid action. The complexity produces more nuanced, better-calibrated decisions, not indefinite deferral. You can say "this is genuinely hard" and still move toward a decision with integrity.

**Paralysis by moral complexity:** "It's complicated" becomes a permanent response to moral demands. The recognition of multiple valid frameworks becomes a reason never to commit to a position, never to take a principled stand, and never to accept responsibility for a decision. Complexity functions as moral camouflage.

**Diagnostic question:** *Is my recognition of moral complexity moving me toward a more nuanced decision, or has it become a way of avoiding any decision at all?*

---

### Distinction C: Sophisticated moral reasoning vs. moral courage

**Genuine moral imagination with moral courage:** Your sophisticated multi-framework analysis leads to action, including action that carries real cost — social disapproval, relational disruption, personal sacrifice. You do what the integrated analysis requires, not only what is conceptually interesting to discuss.

**Sophisticated moral reasoning without moral courage:** You can articulate integral ethics with fluency. You can map moral tensions with precision. You know what the phronesis-based response would be. You do not act on it. The sophistication of the reasoning is inversely proportional to the willingness to accept its demands.

This is what Narvaez and Rest called the **moral action gap at the integral level** — not the gap between Stage 3 and Stage 6, but the gap between Stage 6 analysis and the character strength required to act on it.

**Diagnostic question:** *Is there an action my integrated analysis clearly requires that I have been avoiding? What is the real reason I have not taken it?*
