This module addresses one of the most consequential intersections on the platform: moral development and shadow work. You will identify stage-specific moral shadow patterns, distinguish moral injury from ordinary guilt and from PTSD, and build practices that close the gap between your convictions and your actions.

## 🧭 Orient

## Moral shadow: working definition

**Moral shadow** is the disowned moral capacities and commitments that have been **projected, suppressed, or bypassed**. In practice, this means a person condemns in others what they have not integrated in themselves, or absolutizes one moral strength while disowning another that development now requires.

## Stage-specific moral shadow patterns

### Conventional level shadow

| Pattern | Shadow expression | Developmental reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage 4 suppresses Stage 2 self-interest** | Corruption, rationalized rule-bending, "exception for me" logic while publicly defending law-and-order | Legitimate duty orientation is present, but disowned self-interest leaks out covertly |
| **Stage 3 suppresses moral agency** | Passive resentment, indirect sabotage, compliance-with-anger instead of direct moral voice | Relational loyalty is overused; agency is disowned to preserve approval and belonging |

### Postconventional level shadow

| Pattern | Shadow expression | Developmental reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage 5/6 suppresses conventional relational care** | Moral arrogance, relational callousness, contempt in service of "principle" | Principled clarity is real, but care-track capacities are bypassed rather than included |

## Moral injury: from combat formulation to wider contexts

Jonathan Shay's original formulation of moral injury in combat veterans names a distinct wound: profound violation of what is right by a legitimate authority in high-stakes conditions. Subsequent literature extends moral injury to civilian settings, including medical professionals, educators, organizational actors, and any person who has acted against their deepest moral convictions (or was made complicit without full understanding).

Moral injury is related to trauma but not identical to PTSD, and it is not reducible to ordinary guilt. PTSD centers fear-based threat conditioning; guilt is typically "I did something wrong." Moral injury centers violation of moral identity, trust, and meaning, often with shame, grief, and betrayal.

## (a) Moral Shadow Inventory (3-2-1 adapted to moral projection)

Choose one person or group whose moral failings trigger your strongest contempt.

1. **Face It (3rd person):** Describe the moral failing in concrete terms. What do they violate? What do you judge most strongly?
2. **Talk to It (2nd person):** Address the pattern directly: "You are..." Ask what this pattern protects, fears, or seeks.
3. **Be It (1st person):** Speak as the disowned pattern: "I am the part of me that..."

Complete:

- Whose moral failings most trigger your contempt?
- What does this reveal about your own suppressed moral patterns?
- Which stage-specific shadow pattern (Stage 3, Stage 4, Stage 5/6) is most active for you now?

## (b) Moral Injury Acknowledgment Practice

Use this journaling protocol to acknowledge and metabolize moral injury without collapsing into shame.

| Prompt | Your response |
|---|---|
| What happened? (facts only) | |
| What moral conviction was violated? | |
| What role did coercion, hierarchy, or limited understanding play? | |
| What emotions are present now (grief, anger, shame, betrayal, numbness)? | |
| What support or repair is needed next? | |

**Critical distinction to write explicitly:**

- **Guilt:** "I did something wrong."
- **Moral injury:** "I was made complicit in a moral violation against my will or without my full understanding" (or under impossible constraints).

End with one sentence of non-shaming accountability: *"The next honest step I can take is..."*

## (c) Moral Courage Inventory (conviction-action gap)

Map the gap between what you say you believe and what you actually do. Treat this as developmental diagnosis, not self-accusation.

| Domain | My moral conviction (what I believe) | My recurrent action (what I do) | Gap size (low/med/high) | Likely blocker (fear, role pressure, belonging, fatigue, ambiguity) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Work | | | | |
| Relationships | | | | |
| Civic/community | | | | |

Then write 5-8 sentences:

1. Which gap is most developmentally important right now?
2. What one "next courage action" would reduce that gap this week?
3. What support increases follow-through probability?

## 📚 Learn

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

- Define moral shadow as disowned moral capacities and commitments that are projected, suppressed, or bypassed.
- Identify conventional and postconventional stage-specific moral shadow patterns.
- Distinguish guilt, PTSD-related distress, and moral injury with greater precision.
- Apply a moral-projection 3-2-1 process to your strongest moral contempt trigger.
- Map and act on one conviction-action gap as a moral courage practice.

## 🧘 Practice

Use this 7-day protocol:

- **Day 1:** Complete Orient and identify your dominant shadow pattern.
- **Day 2:** Complete Encounter (a) Moral Shadow Inventory.
- **Day 3:** Complete Encounter (b) Moral Injury Acknowledgment Practice.
- **Day 4:** Complete Encounter (c) Moral Courage Inventory.
- **Day 5:** Revisit your highest-gap domain and design one concrete courage action.
- **Day 6:** Take the courage action in a bounded, safe context.
- **Day 7:** Debrief in writing: what changed in your moral clarity, care, and accountability?

---

## 🧲 Stabilize

Use this checkpoint before strong moral action:

1. **Shadow check:** Am I condemning in others what I have not yet owned in myself?
2. **Care check:** Is my principle bypassing relational care?
3. **Complicity check:** Am I naming my real constraints honestly, without self-exoneration theater?
4. **Courage check:** What concrete action follows from my stated conviction within 72 hours?

---

## 🆘 When to Seek Support

Moral injury has genuine clinical dimensions. Seek qualified **therapeutic, pastoral, or trauma-informed professional support** if one or more of the following persist:

- Intrusive memories, nightmares, or severe distress tied to moral violation events.
- Persistent shame, self-condemnation, or loss of meaning that does not ease with reflection.
- Isolation, withdrawal, or inability to trust self/others after a moral violation.
- Ongoing rage, numbness, or despair that impairs work, relationships, or basic functioning.
- Thoughts of self-harm, hopelessness, or inability to stay safe.

If immediate safety is a concern, contact local emergency/crisis resources now.
