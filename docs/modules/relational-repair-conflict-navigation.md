## 🧠 Learn

This module trains the capacity to navigate interpersonal rupture — not to avoid conflict, but to move through it in ways that strengthen rather than erode relational trust.

### 1. Rupture and Repair as the Developmental Engine of Relational Trust

Every sustained relationship contains ruptures — moments when attunement breaks down, expectations collide, needs fail to be met, or trust is strained. This is not a sign of relational failure. Research on infant–caregiver development (Tronick, 1989; Siegel, 2012) demonstrates that the developmental engine of relational trust is not continuous attunement but the repeated cycle of **misattunement → distress → repair**.

Three features of this model are significant for adult relational practice:

- **Repair builds what attunement alone cannot.** A relationship in which ruptures never occur — or are never acknowledged — does not build the deep relational trust that comes from knowing another person can be with you in difficulty and return. What makes repair developmental is that it requires both parties to tolerate vulnerability, acknowledge impact, and choose reconnection. That process, repeated over time, is what actually builds relational resilience.

- **Chronic unrepaired rupture erodes trust non-linearly.** Research on couples (Gottman, 1994) shows that it is not the presence of conflict but the absence of repair that predicts relationship deterioration. A high-conflict relationship with consistent repair can be stable and growth-producing. A low-conflict relationship with unrepaired accumulations can deteriorate silently until breakdown.

- **The capacity to initiate repair is a developmental achievement.** At earlier developmental stages, repair initiation feels like capitulation, humiliation, or an admission of fault. The recognition that repair is a relational act — not a verdict about who was wrong — requires the structural capacity to hold one's own position *and* the relational field simultaneously. This is an Interpersonal Line L3/L4 capacity.

### 2. Gottman's Four Horsemen and the Repair Attempt

John Gottman's decades of observational research on couples (1994, 1999) identified four behavioral patterns that, when chronic, reliably predict relational breakdown. He called these the **Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse:**

| Horseman | What it looks like | What it communicates | Its antidote |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Criticism** | Attacking the partner's character rather than describing the specific behavior ("You always...", "You never...", "You're so...") | "Something is fundamentally wrong with you as a person" | Gentle start-up: describe the specific behavior and your feeling/need |
| **Contempt** | Mockery, sarcasm, eye-rolling, name-calling, treating the other as beneath consideration | "I am superior to you" — the most predictive of the four for relationship dissolution | Building a culture of appreciation; repairing fondness and admiration |
| **Defensiveness** | Counterattacking, cross-complaining, victimizing yourself to deflect responsibility | "The problem isn't me, it's you" | Taking responsibility; acknowledging your part even when the complaint isn't entirely fair |
| **Stonewalling** | Shutting down, withdrawing, flat affect, going silent — often a response to emotional flooding | "I am no longer here" — physiologically: the person has typically flooded (heart rate >100 bpm) | Physiological self-soothing; taking a break of at least 20 minutes; returning to the conversation |

> **Critical use note:** These four patterns are diagnostic of *process*, not character. The purpose of learning the Four Horsemen is to identify patterns in your own behavior — not to catalogue the other person's. Using this framework to build a case against a partner is itself a form of contempt. The antidote always begins with self-observation.

**The Repair Attempt** is Gottman's term for any action — verbal or nonverbal — that interrupts an escalating conflict cycle and invites de-escalation. Research shows that the presence of repair attempts, and critically the *partner's willingness to accept them*, is one of the strongest predictors of relationship health. A repair attempt can be as simple as: *"I'm getting flooded — can we take a break?"* or *"I know I didn't say that well. Can I try again?"* or even a joke that cuts the tension.

Gottman's research also identified the **5:1 ratio**: stable relationships tend to maintain approximately five positive interactions for every negative one. In conflict, the ratio temporarily drops, but in healthy relationships it recovers. Chronic ratios below 1:1 predict deterioration. The implication: relational repair is not just crisis intervention — it is the ongoing practice of maintaining a sufficient positive relational account.

### 3. The Repair Sequence: Acknowledgement → Accountability → Amends → Reconnection

Research on rupture and repair in therapeutic relationships (Safran & Muran, 2000) and in everyday relational life converges on a four-phase sequence for effective repair:

**Acknowledgement:** *"I can see that what happened affected you."* Repair begins with acknowledgement of impact — not of intent, not of whether you think the person's response is proportionate, but of the fact that something happened that mattered. Acknowledgement without qualification is the entry point. Immediately moving to explanation or justification blocks acknowledgement: *"I can see you're upset, but the reason I did that was..."* is not acknowledgement — it is a transition to self-defence.

**Accountability:** *"I contributed to this."* Accountability is specific: it names the actual behavior, not a generalised self-criticism. *"I raised my voice and that wasn't okay"* is accountability. *"I'm just a terrible communicator"* is self-deprecation that actually deflects accountability by centering the speaker's feelings about themselves rather than the impact on the other person. Accountability does not require total agreement with the other person's account of events. It requires identifying the part of the rupture that belonged to you.

**Amends:** *"Here is what I intend to do differently."* Amends close the gap between acknowledgement and future behavior. Without amends, acknowledgement can become a repeated pattern of apology without change — what Dan Wile calls the *apology loop*. Amends are behavioral and specific: not *"I'll try to be better"* but *"When I notice I'm getting flooded in our conversations, I will ask for a break before I escalate."*

**Reconnection:** *"I value this relationship."* The repair sequence closes by affirming the relational bond that the conflict threatened. Reconnection is not compulsory — in some conflicts, the honest position is uncertainty about the relationship. But where repair is genuine, some acknowledgement that the relationship matters — expressed in a form that is authentic, not formulaic — is the closing step that shifts the relational field from rupture to continuity.

### 4. NVC as a Practical Repair Tool

Marshall Rosenberg's **Nonviolent Communication** (NVC) framework provides a four-component communication structure that is particularly useful in repair contexts:

| NVC Component | What it involves | Example in repair context |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Observation** | Describe the specific behavior, without evaluation or interpretation | "When you left the meeting without responding to my question..." |
| **Feeling** | Name the emotion in the speaker — not a thought, interpretation, or accusation | "...I felt embarrassed and disconnected..." |
| **Need** | Identify the underlying need the situation failed to meet | "...because I need to feel like my contributions are seen..." |
| **Request** | Make a specific, actionable, present-tense request — not a demand | "...and I'm wondering if you'd be willing to tell me what was happening for you in that moment." |

> **A crucial framing note:** NVC is a practical communication tool — a structured approach to first-person communication that reduces blame and increases the likelihood of being genuinely heard. It is not a developmental framework and does not map to any particular AQAL stage. It can be used mechanically or authentically; its effectiveness depends on genuine presence and self-knowledge, not syntactic correctness. The formulaic application of NVC in conflict — *"When you did X, I felt Y"* repeated defensively — misses the point. The framework's value is in training the speaker's attention toward their own interior: What am I actually feeling? What do I actually need? These questions are themselves developmental exercises.

The NVC framework is most useful in repair when applied as a *preparation tool* — before the conversation rather than during it. Working out what you actually felt, what need was at stake, and what you are actually asking for, before engaging the other person, significantly increases the quality of the repair attempt.

### 5. Power Dynamics in Conflict

Conflict is not experienced symmetrically. Three structural dimensions shape the asymmetry:

**Developmental stage:** A person at Institutional altitude (Kegan 4) and a person at Interpersonal altitude (Kegan 3) navigating the same conflict are not in a symmetrical encounter. The Kegan 3 person's sense of self is more deeply embedded in relational harmony; conflict feels more existentially threatening; the pressure to restore relational connection can lead to premature agreement that forecloses genuine repair. The Kegan 4 person may read this as resolution; the Kegan 3 person may have merely accommodated. Awareness of stage asymmetry does not mean treating the other as less-than; it means attending to whether genuine voice is present on both sides.

**Role and institutional power:** In organisational contexts, hierarchical position radically shapes conflict navigation. The person with less institutional power faces a different risk calculus: raising a concern to a supervisor, naming a conflict publicly, initiating repair with someone who has authority over their livelihood — all carry material stakes that the higher-power party typically does not carry to the same degree. Amy Edmondson's research on psychological safety demonstrates that interpersonal risk-taking — including repair attempts — is directly shaped by the organisational environment and the perceived safety of speaking up. Repair cannot be practiced in a vacuum; it requires conditions.

**Social identity:** Conflicts that cross lines of race, gender, class, disability, nationality, or other social identity categories carry differential stakes for the parties involved. The person from a historically marginalized group navigating conflict with someone from a dominant group is not in a symmetrical encounter, even when both parties are operating with good intentions. The asymmetry is structural — it predates the specific conflict and shapes what each party can safely say, how their contributions are heard, and what the consequences of breakdown are likely to be. Naming this asymmetry is not to resolve it; it is to be honest about the terrain.

**Implications for practice:** In any conflict, it is worth asking: Who has more to lose from this conflict? Who has more institutional, social, or relational power in this context? Whose repair attempt would require more courage or carry more risk? The answer shapes the ethical dimensions of what repair looks like and who carries what responsibility for initiating it.

### 6. Stage-Relative Conflict Patterns

Developmental stage does not determine whether a person experiences conflict — everyone does. It shapes the *meaning* conflict carries and the range of responses available.

**Conformist / Amber — Conflict as rule violation:**
At Conformist altitude, belonging to the group and conforming to its norms are the anchors of identity and safety. Conflict is experienced as a threat to group cohesion and as evidence that someone has violated the rules that hold the community together. The default response is either compliance (restore harmony by agreeing, even without genuine resolution) or exclusion (the conflicting person is wrong, deviant, or must be expelled). The shadow is conflict avoidance: difficult things are not named because naming them would violate the relational harmony that is the primary value. Repair at this altitude often looks like resuming normal behavior without acknowledgement — *"Let's just move on."* What is avoided is the acknowledgement that would require admitting that a rule was broken or that the relationship has complexity that the group's norms cannot contain.

**Achiever / Orange — Conflict as problem to be solved:**
At Achiever altitude, effectiveness and outcomes are the central organising values. Conflict is a problem: it wastes time, reduces productivity, and gets in the way of the goal. The default response is solution-finding: *"Let's identify what went wrong, fix it, and move forward."* This is a genuine advance over avoidance — conflict is addressed rather than suppressed. The shadow is that the solution-focus can bypass the relational and emotional dimensions of conflict. The other person's feelings, the impact of the rupture on trust, and the quality of the relational field are secondary to getting back to work. Repair at this altitude can be functionally effective while remaining relationally thin: the problem is solved but the person is not quite seen.

**Strategist / Teal — Conflict as developmental opportunity:**
At Strategist / early Teal altitude, there is sufficient self-authoring capacity and meta-awareness to hold conflict as information rather than threat. Conflict reveals the points where values, needs, or worldviews genuinely differ — and those differences are potentially as generative as they are uncomfortable. The repair sequence is navigable not because conflict is no longer activating but because the person has developed the structural capacity to be activated *and* to observe that activation, to hold their own position *and* to remain genuinely curious about the other's experience. The shadow at this altitude is intellectualization: the genuine developmental interest in conflict-as-information can become a defense against the felt impact of the rupture. *"I'm curious about our different perspectives"* can be true and also be a way of avoiding the acknowledgement that something actually hurt.

> **Practical use:** These stage descriptions are not a typology for sorting other people. They are a mirror for examining your own default patterns: Where do you recognise yourself? Which conflict behaviors do you engage in automatically? The developmental work is not to judge the pattern but to expand the repertoire.

## 🧘 Practice

**Duration:** 25–30 minutes for the full sequence. Each exercise can be completed separately.

**Setup:** Private space. Journal or paper. Choose a specific past conflict — one that is resolved or sufficiently historical that you can reflect on it without significant current activation. If reviewing a particular conflict raises your current distress above a 6/10, choose a different, lower-charge example for this practice.

> ⚠️ **Before beginning:** These practices are for *reflection on past conflicts*, not for use in an active conflict. Working through these exercises prepares you for future repair conversations — it does not substitute for them. See the Safety Note before proceeding.

---

### Practice A — Four Horsemen Self-Assessment (10 minutes)

Review your chosen past conflict. Working through each of the Four Horsemen, reflect on your own behavior — not the other person's.

**Step 1 — Criticism:** In this conflict, did you make any statements about the other person's character rather than describing specific behavior? (Look for: "You always...", "You never...", "You're so...", "The problem with you is..."). Write down any instances.

**Step 2 — Contempt:** Did you express contempt — through mockery, sarcasm, dismissiveness, or treating the other person as less capable or less worthy of respect? (This can be subtle: an eye-roll, a sigh that communicates *you're being ridiculous*, a tone of patient condescension.) Write down any instances.

**Step 3 — Defensiveness:** When the other person raised a concern or complaint, did you immediately counterattack, deflect, or locate the problem back with them before genuinely hearing the concern? Write down any instances.

**Step 4 — Stonewalling:** Did you withdraw — go silent, leave the room, shut down emotionally, become expressionless or flat? (This is often a response to flooding rather than a conscious choice. Note whether you were flooded — heart racing, difficulty thinking — at the time.) Write down any instances.

**Step 5 — Reflection:** Which Horseman showed up most prominently in your behavior? What does that pattern tell you about your default conflict response? (Not as self-criticism — as information about the edge of your current repertoire.)

---

### Practice B — Repair Attempt Design (10 minutes)

For the same conflict, design a repair attempt using the four-step sequence.

**Step 1 — Acknowledgement:** Write one to three sentences that acknowledge the impact of the rupture on the other person — without justification, explanation, or qualifying clauses. What did they experience? What did the rupture cost the relationship?

**Step 2 — Accountability:** Write one to two sentences naming your specific contribution to the rupture — the behavior(s) that belonged to you. Be specific. Avoid either self-deprecation ("I'm just bad at this") or over-qualification ("I did X, but only because you...").

**Step 3 — Amends:** Write one specific, behavioral intention for how you would navigate this situation differently. Not an aspiration — a concrete behavior change. ("When I notice I'm getting flooded, I will ask for a 20-minute break before continuing.")

**Step 4 — Reconnection:** Write one sentence that expresses what this relationship means to you, in authentic terms. This need not be warm or effusive — it should be honest. What is the relational value at stake?

**Step 5 — Review:** Read back what you have written. Does it feel honest and specific? Where do you notice hedging, over-explanation, or deflection? Revise as needed until it reads like something you could actually say to this person.

---

### Practice C — NVC Translation (5 minutes)

Take one statement from the conflict — something you said, or something you wanted to say — and translate it into NVC format.

**Step 1 — Original statement:** Write the original statement (or internal thought) as it actually occurred.

**Step 2 — Observation:** Separate out the observable behavior that was the trigger — only what a neutral observer could have seen or heard, with no interpretation or evaluation.

**Step 3 — Feeling:** Name what you actually felt — the emotion in your own interior. Not "I felt that you...", not "I felt attacked/dismissed/misunderstood" (these are interpretations of the other's intent) — but the basic emotion: scared, sad, angry, hurt, ashamed, confused, lonely.

**Step 4 — Need:** Identify the underlying need that the situation failed to meet. Needs are universal human needs — not strategies or preferences: connection, respect, safety, clarity, autonomy, contribution, fairness.

**Step 5 — Request:** Write a specific, actionable, present-tense request — something the other person could actually do. Check that it is a request (which allows for "no") rather than a demand (which does not).

**Step 6 — Reflection:** Compare the original statement with the NVC translation. What changed? What was hidden in the original that the translation surfaces? What does this tell you about what you were actually trying to communicate?

## 🔄 Integrate

This module's practices prepare the *interior* of repair — the self-awareness and communication clarity needed to enter a repair conversation. Integration happens in the actual relational encounter.

**This week:** Identify one relationship where a small unaddressed rupture exists — something relatively low-charge (3/10 or below). Use the repair sequence from Practice B as preparation and initiate repair. Note what happens.

**Ongoing:** Before any significant conflict conversation, take 5 minutes to work through the NVC translation of what you want to say. Notice whether clarifying your own observation, feeling, need, and request changes the quality of the conversation.

**For facilitators:** This module is Tier 2 (see Safety Note). If using in a group or coaching context, review the Facilitator Note below before proceeding.

## 🗒️ Facilitator Note

**Timing:** Full module in facilitated group: 90–120 minutes (30 minutes for Learn with discussion, 60 minutes for Practices A–C, 30 minutes for debrief). Individual coaching context: 60 minutes. Practices A and B should be completed in sequence; Practice C can stand alone.

**Watch-fors:**

- **Current conflict activation:** Participants may select a past conflict that is still emotionally raw. Watch for elevated activation (rapid speech, flat affect, tearfulness, anger directed at absent third party) as a signal that the chosen conflict is above the recommended charge level. Invite the participant to select a lower-charge example.
- **Four Horsemen self-assessment as weaponisation:** Some participants will use the Horsemen framework to build a case against the other party rather than examining their own behavior. Redirect explicitly: "We're using this to look at your own patterns — what do you notice in yourself?"
- **Accountability as self-flagellation:** The accountability step can trigger shame responses that become the focus of the exercise rather than relational repair. Hold the distinction: specific accountability is useful; global self-criticism is not.
- **Power asymmetries in the group:** In groups with significant hierarchical or social-identity differences, the repair sequence and NVC exercises can be experienced very differently by participants with different levels of institutional power. Name this explicitly rather than treating the exercises as universally applicable.

(2015). *Nonviolent Communication: A Language of Life* (3rd ed.). PuddleDancer Press | **Tier B** | NVC has substantial practitioner evidence and is widely used in conflict resolution, organisational, and therapeutic contexts. Controlled trials specifically evaluating the four-component NVC framework are limited and methodologically heterogeneous. Available evidence on NVC-based training shows positive effects on communication quality, empathy, and conflict outcomes in some contexts (Marlow et al., 2012; Kimiecik et al., 2022); effect sizes vary. NVC is cited here as a practical communication tool, not a developmental framework. Users should not expect that syntactic application of the framework produces outcomes independently of genuine presence and self-knowledge. |

Harvard Business School Press | **Tier B** | Kegan's subject–object theory has substantial theoretical coherence and longitudinal developmental research support. The Conformist / Achiever / Strategist conflict archetypes described here are theoretically derived and have practitioner resonance; they are not empirically validated as discrete, experimentally measurable conflict styles. |

## 🩺 Safety Note

**Tier:** 2 — Conflict material can be highly activating. Past-conflict framing required. Facilitator-supported debrief recommended for group use.

---

> ### ⚠️ Before you begin
>
> This module's practices are designed exclusively for **reflection on past conflicts** — not for use in, during, or immediately preceding an active conflict. The exercises prepare the interior conditions for repair; they do not substitute for the repair conversation itself, and using them as real-time scripts in an active conflict is likely to be counterproductive.
>
> Select a conflict that is sufficiently historical or resolved that you can reflect on it from a position of relative stability. If thinking about the conflict raises your current distress or activation level above **6/10**, choose a lower-charge example before proceeding.
>
> ---
>
> ### This may not be the right moment if:
>
> - You are currently in an acute conflict that is escalating, involving legal proceedings, or involving safety concerns.
> - You are in a period of significant emotional distress unrelated to this module's content.
> - The relationship you are considering involves current abuse, coercion, or a significant threat to your physical or emotional safety. **This module is not designed for use in abusive relationship contexts.** If you are in a relationship involving abuse, please reach out to an appropriate support service.
> - You are currently processing a relational trauma with a therapist and that work is not yet stabilized.
>
> ---
>
> ### Stop the exercise and ground yourself if you notice:
>
> - Emotional flooding — rapid heartbeat, difficulty thinking, tearfulness that does not settle, intense anger that does not resolve.
> - Intrusive memories or images from the conflict that are difficult to step back from.
> - Dissociation — feeling detached from your body, the room, or a sense of unreality.
> - An overwhelming impulse to contact the other party immediately, send a message, or confront the conflict right now (this module is not preparation for a contact decision — it is preparation for an eventual intentional one).
>
> **If you need to stop:** Place both feet flat on the floor. Look slowly around the room and name five things you can see. Breathe unhurriedly. When more settled, you may continue, close the exercises, or choose to return another day. All are valid.
>
> ---
>
> ### Facilitator-supported debrief requirement (group settings)
>
> When these practices are conducted in a group, workshop, or facilitated learning context, **a qualified facilitator must be present throughout and must lead a structured debrief.** Group use of conflict material carries non-trivial interpersonal risk — participants may be reminded of shared conflicts within the group itself, and the material can surface relational vulnerability that affects the group as a whole.
>
> Facilitators should:
> - Brief participants explicitly before the exercises: these are *past* conflict exercises; the material is not an invitation to resolve a current interpersonal issue within the group.
> - Allow participants to decline any exercise without explanation.
> - Monitor for visible activation and check in with activated participants privately before any whole-group sharing.
> - Allow voluntary, not required, whole-group sharing.
> - Do not conduct these exercises in a group where a live, unresolved conflict between participants exists — without additional safeguards and explicit consent from all parties.
>
> ---
>
> ### If distress continues:
>
> If you remain significantly distressed after closing the exercises and using grounding resources, please reach out to a qualified mental health professional or qualified mediator. This module is educational content; it is not a substitute for clinical support or professional conflict mediation.
>
> ---
