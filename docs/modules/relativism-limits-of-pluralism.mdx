**Growing Up path:** This module addresses the structural *ceiling* of Pluralistic/Green — the point at which Green's own epistemology turns back on itself and reveals its incompleteness. The Amber worldview's ceiling was the inability to handle genuine pluralism. Orange's ceiling was the inability to recognize the cultural and perspectival embeddedness of its own "universal" reason. Green's ceiling is the inability to provide any ground for action, evaluation, or discernment — because its epistemology, taken to its logical conclusion, disallows any meta-perspective that could make such judgments possible. Recognizing this ceiling is not a defeat. It is Green's most important developmental achievement.

## 🧭 Orient

## The Most Important Question in This Module

Here is the philosophical problem you will be working with:

*If all perspectives are equally valid, then the perspective that "all perspectives are equally valid" is only one perspective among others — and therefore no more valid than the perspective that some perspectives are better than others.*

This is not a clever debater's trick. It is a genuine structural problem — what philosophers call a **performative contradiction**: a claim that undermines itself in the act of being asserted. A performative contradiction occurs when the truth of a statement would require the falsity of the conditions under which it is being asserted.

The version that concerns this module: radical pluralism — the view that all perspectives are equally valid and that no meta-perspective is available to evaluate them — is itself a meta-perspective being asserted as more valid than its rivals. The pluralist who sincerely believes all perspectives are equal is, in the act of asserting this, claiming their pluralism is better than the alternative. They are privileging a perspective while claiming no perspective should be privileged.

This is Green's characteristic blind spot. It is not a minor inconsistency. It is the structural ceiling of the Pluralistic/Green stage.

---

## Why This Matters: What Green Cannot Do

The practical consequences of this structural limitation are significant and observable.

**Green cannot adjudicate genuine value conflicts.** If all perspectives are equally valid, what happens when one culture values female autonomy and another values female subordination? Green's epistemology offers two exits: relativism ("both are equally valid within their own context") and contradiction (covertly asserting that female autonomy is better while claiming no such assessment is possible). Neither is adequate. The first abandons women in subordinating cultures to their context. The second performs the very hierarchy Green claims to reject.

**Green cannot ground activism.** Activist commitment — the willingness to act, to advocate, to organize, to risk — presupposes that some things matter more than others, that some outcomes are better than others, that action in service of justice is better than inaction. This is a hierarchical judgment. It cannot be made coherently from a position of radical relativism. Yet Green communities produce many of the world's most committed activists. The epistemology and the practice are in constant unacknowledged tension.

**Green cannot protect itself from its own shadow.** The characteristic shadow of Green communities — fierce in-group policing of members who deviate from pluralist norms, the paradox of "tolerating everything except intolerance" — is precisely the performative contradiction made organizational. Green collectives become, structurally, the most judgmental environments precisely because they cannot acknowledge that they are making judgments. The judgments go underground and emerge as social pressure, shaming, and exclusion rather than as explicit reasoning.

**Green cannot acknowledge development.** If all perspectives are equally valid, then the view that "human development occurs in a rough sequence of increasing complexity and adequacy" is only equally as valid as the view that it does not. But this is precisely the insight Green itself represents: a recognition that Orange's universalism is not the final word, that multiple frames exist, that the knowing of the knower shapes what is known. Green arrived at this insight through development. But its epistemology cannot justify the value of that arrival.

## Wilber's Diagnosis: Aperspectival Madness

Ken Wilber, drawing on Jean Gebser's analysis of consciousness structures, names this condition **aperspectival madness** — the pathological endpoint of the postmodern insight that all knowing is perspectival.

Gebser's analysis of consciousness structures traces a developmental sequence in which each structure brings a new relationship to perspective. The magical structure has no clear distinction between self and world, between subject and object. The mythic structure organizes reality around a central narrative and membership in a community. The mental/rational structure achieves the capacity for formal perspective-taking: the ability to stand apart from one's immediate experience and evaluate it against universal criteria. The integral/aperspectival structure, as Gebser envisions it, achieves a new kind of transparency: the capacity to hold multiple perspectives simultaneously without collapsing into any one of them, while retaining the ability to act.

The failure mode of the transition between rational and integral structures is what Gebser calls **aperspectivalism** — the breakdown of the capacity for perspective into a paralysis of perspectives. Instead of achieving the genuine transparency of integral consciousness (which Gebser calls *diaphaneity*), the mind gets stuck at the recognition that all perspectives are perspectival and concludes from this that no perspective can be used to evaluate any other.

Wilber identifies this as the characteristic failure mode of postmodern academic philosophy and of Green organizational cultures. The diagnosis: the postmodern recognition that all knowing is perspectival — a genuine and important insight — is taken to mean that no perspective can be evaluated as more or less adequate, comprehensive, or developmentally mature than any other. What was a liberating insight (all knowing is contextual and partial) becomes a new absolutism (therefore no meta-evaluation is possible), which is itself a hidden absolutism — an absolute prohibition on hierarchy.

This is not a dismissal of postmodernism's genuine contributions. The postmodern critique of naive universalism is valid and important. The problem is when the critique becomes the final word rather than a stage in an ongoing conversation.

## Three Positions: A Map

To navigate this territory clearly, it helps to distinguish three positions that are often conflated in popular discourse.

### Position 1: Orange Universalism

**The claim:** There are universal truths, universal values, and universal standards of rationality that apply to all human beings regardless of cultural context. Science provides the method; reason provides the arbiter; evidence decides disputes. Cultural differences are either variations on universal themes or errors to be corrected by education and evidence.

**What is right about this:** Formal operational reasoning does produce genuinely cross-cultural insights. Scientific method does generate knowledge that is not merely culturally relative — vaccines work regardless of cultural context; the evidence for human-caused climate change does not vary with the politics of the observer. Some values — freedom from arbitrary violence, basic epistemic access, non-deception — can be defended across cultural contexts with universal-aspiring arguments.

**What is limited about this:** It cannot adequately acknowledge the extent to which "universal reason" has historically been one culture's reasoning presented as universal. The Enlightenment's "universal man" was frequently a specific kind of European, educated, propertied man. Scientific rationality, deployed without cultural awareness, has been used to justify colonialism, eugenics, and the systematic dismissal of non-Western knowledge traditions. Orange's universalism, in its unreflective form, confuses the structure of formal reasoning with the parochial content it has sometimes carried.

### Position 2: Green Relativism

**The claim:** All perspectives are valid within their own frame of reference. No meta-perspective is available that would allow us to evaluate one cultural, ethical, or epistemological framework as better or worse than another. Truth is perspectival; reason is culturally embedded; what counts as evidence is itself shaped by prior frameworks. The appropriate response to genuine difference is radical inclusion: holding space for all perspectives, resisting hierarchical ranking, replacing evaluation with curiosity.

**What is right about this:** It is a genuine developmental advance over Orange universalism. The recognition that all knowing is perspectival — that the knower's position, culture, body, and history shape what they can know and how they know it — is an important and well-supported insight. It opens the door to genuine pluralism: the capacity to take seriously the knowledge claims of traditions other than one's own. Green's commitment to including marginalized voices, to questioning whose "universal" is being universalized, to attending to power differentials in the production of knowledge — these are real and important contributions.

**What is limited about this:** The performative contradiction described above. Green relativism cannot ground its own most important commitments. It cannot coherently assert that the perspective that "no perspective is better than any other" is better than the perspective that some perspectives are better. It cannot ground activism, adjudicate value conflicts, protect against its own shadow, or acknowledge the developmental insight on which it is itself based.

### Position 3: Integral Perspectivism

**The claim:** All perspectives are valid *and* some perspectives are more comprehensive than others. Both of these are true, and the apparent tension between them is resolved when the two claims are seen to operate at different levels.

Every perspective captures something real. The Amber mythic worldview captures genuine truths about community, meaning, and belonging that Orange rationalism can miss. The Orange rational worldview captures genuine truths about empirical method, individual autonomy, and logical consistency that Amber can miss. The Green pluralistic worldview captures genuine truths about cultural embeddedness, the partiality of all knowing, and the importance of including marginalized perspectives that Orange can miss. *No perspective is simply wrong.*

And yet: a perspective that can hold all of the above simultaneously — acknowledging the genuine contributions of Amber, Orange, and Green while also seeing the limitations of each — is *more comprehensive* than any single one of them. It has not negated the others; it has included and transcended them. This is the difference between a **dominator hierarchy** (where higher means the others are crushed, excluded, or despised) and an **actualization hierarchy** (where higher means the others are included, honored, and integrated into a larger whole).

The move from Green to Integral is not the abandonment of Green's pluralistic values. It is the expansion of the epistemological ground that makes those values coherent and actionable.

## Dominator Hierarchies vs. Actualization Hierarchies

One of the deepest confusions in Green's relationship to hierarchy is the conflation of two fundamentally different kinds of hierarchy.

**Dominator hierarchies** are arrangements in which higher levels of the hierarchy exercise power over lower levels through coercion, exclusion, exploitation, or violence. The master-slave relationship is a dominator hierarchy. Patriarchy as a social structure is a dominator hierarchy. Colonialism is a dominator hierarchy. These arrangements are rightly criticized and rejected; the inequality they produce is not justified by any genuine difference in complexity or comprehension, but by the exercise of power. Green's rejection of these structures is one of its most important contributions.

**Actualization hierarchies** (also called growth hierarchies or developmental hierarchies) are arrangements in which higher levels include and integrate lower levels, and in which the "higher" designation refers to greater complexity, comprehensiveness, or organizational capacity — not to the right to dominate. A cell is more complex than a molecule; an organism is more complex than a cell; an ecosystem is more complex than an organism. In none of these cases does the higher level "dominate" the lower — it includes and depends on the lower levels. Destroy the molecules and you destroy the cells; destroy the cells and you destroy the organism.

The same structure applies to developmental stages. The capacity for formal operational reasoning (Orange) includes and transcends concrete operational reasoning (Amber) — it does not destroy it but incorporates it into a larger capacity. Green perspectivism includes and transcends Orange universalism — it does not eliminate the capacity for formal reasoning but contextualizes and enriches it.

Green's rejection of dominator hierarchies is justified and important. Green's rejection of *all* hierarchies — including developmental actualization hierarchies — is the structural error that produces aperspectival madness. When every hierarchy is read as a dominator hierarchy, the genuine distinctions of complexity, comprehensiveness, and developmental capacity become invisible — and the epistemological ground disappears.

## 🌿 Encounter

## Practice A — Performative Contradiction Inventory (30–40 minutes)

**Purpose:** To notice — with intellectual honesty and without self-punishment — the moments when your own pluralistic commitments produce hierarchical judgments that you then disown, suppress, or render invisible.

**What a performative contradiction looks like in practice:** A pluralist asserts "all cultures are equally valid" and then expresses moral outrage at a culture that practices female genital mutilation. The outrage is a hierarchical judgment — an assertion that one cultural practice is worse than another. The pluralist is making a judgment they simultaneously claim is impossible. This is not hypocrisy in the ordinary sense; it is the performative contradiction of the position itself, showing up in real behavior.

**Instructions:**

Read each scenario below. For each one, write honestly:
1. What is your initial felt response? (Outrage, discomfort, ambivalence, agreement, confusion?)
2. Does your response involve a hierarchical judgment — an assertion that one perspective, practice, or value is better or worse than another?
3. If yes: what is the hierarchy you are implicitly asserting?
4. Is that hierarchy a dominator hierarchy (based on power/exclusion) or an actualization hierarchy (based on genuine complexity/comprehensiveness)?
5. Can you hold your hierarchical judgment and your commitment to pluralism simultaneously without contradiction — or does something need to shift?

**Scenario 1:** A traditional community practices arranged marriage, and the young adults in that community report generally positive experiences with the practice and endorse it as culturally meaningful. As a pluralist, do you accept this practice as equally valid to practices of individual romantic choice? What if some women in the same community report coercion and distress?

**Scenario 2:** A corporation has a culture of aggressive competition, long working hours, and winner-take-all internal dynamics. Employees who thrive there say they love it. As a pluralist, is this organizational culture equally valid to a collaborative, worker-centered culture? What happens to your pluralism if the competitive culture is producing measurably higher rates of employee burnout?

**Scenario 3:** A political leader publicly asserts that climate change is not real and that environmental regulation destroys freedom. They believe this sincerely. As a pluralist, is their perspective equally valid to a perspective grounded in IPCC consensus? What happens to "all perspectives are equally valid" when one of the perspectives concerns empirical questions with significant evidence?

**Scenario 4:** A spiritual teacher asserts that women's primary calling is in the home, that their spiritual development depends on submission to male authority, and that this is the will of God. Hundreds of sincere practitioners find this teaching deeply meaningful and life-giving. As a pluralist, what is your response?

**Scenario 5:** An anti-pluralist — someone who sincerely and articulately argues that pluralism is morally wrong, that some cultures are genuinely superior to others, and that universal standards of judgment are both possible and necessary — presents their view in a dialogue context. As a pluralist who believes all perspectives deserve inclusion, do you include this perspective in your pluralist holding environment? What happens if you exclude it?

**Scenario 6:** Your own developmental stage — your values, your epistemological commitments, your sense of what is right and good — is itself a perspective among perspectives. From a consistent Green position, the Amber perspective that preceded it, and the Integral perspective that may follow it, are equally valid. Yet you almost certainly do not experience your current values as merely equally valid to Amber's authority-based moral code or Orange's competitive individualism. Where does the hierarchy show up in your own felt sense of your own development?

---

After completing all six scenarios, reflect:

- Which hierarchical judgments were easiest to acknowledge? Which were most uncomfortable?
- Do you notice a pattern in the kinds of hierarchy you are willing to acknowledge vs. the kinds you feel compelled to disown?
- What is at stake — emotionally, relationally, politically — in acknowledging that you make hierarchical judgments?

## Practice B — The Hierarchy Question (25–30 minutes)

**Purpose:** To examine your own relationship to hierarchy specifically, distinguishing between dominator hierarchies (rightly rejected) and actualization hierarchies (necessary for coherent pluralism).

**Background:** Wilber argues that Green's rejection of dominator hierarchies is one of its most significant moral achievements — but that Green has made a categorical error by extending that rejection to all forms of hierarchy. When actualization hierarchies (the nesting of greater within lesser complexity, growth as the expansion of comprehension and inclusion) are rejected along with dominator hierarchies, the epistemological ground for Green's own developmental achievement disappears.

**Instructions:**

**Part 1: Map your hierarchy aversion (10 minutes)**

On a blank page, list every form of hierarchy you currently reject, resist, or experience as morally problematic. Do not filter or edit. Include hierarchies of status, authority, expertise, spiritual development, intelligence, cultural achievement — every hierarchy that your gut-level response identifies as suspect.

**Part 2: Categorize (10 minutes)**

For each hierarchy on your list, ask: Is this a dominator hierarchy (maintained by power, coercion, exclusion, or the systematic devaluation of those at lower levels) or an actualization hierarchy (maintained by genuine differences in complexity, comprehensiveness, or integrative capacity, where the lower levels are included and honored rather than excluded)?

Some will be clearly one or the other. Some will be mixed — genuinely involving both power dynamics and genuine developmental differences. Note these as mixed.

**Part 3: Reflection questions (10 minutes)**

1. Is there any category of hierarchy in which you found it difficult to distinguish dominator from actualization forms? What made the distinction difficult?

2. Have you ever found yourself rejecting an actualization hierarchy (a genuine difference in developmental complexity or expertise) because it superficially resembled a dominator hierarchy? What was the context?

3. If you were to grant that actualization hierarchies are real and valuable — that some forms of knowing genuinely are more comprehensive than others, that some forms of moral reasoning genuinely are more inclusive and consistent than others — what would you have to give up? What would you be able to claim?

4. What does it feel like to apply an actualization-hierarchy lens to developmental stages? Can you acknowledge that Orange is, in certain respects, more cognitively complex than Amber — that it includes and transcends rather than dominating and excluding? Can you acknowledge the same for Green relative to Orange? Can you acknowledge the same for Integral relative to Green?

## Practice C — "What Would You Die For?" Inquiry (30–45 minutes)

**Purpose:** A values-clarification exercise that reveals the non-relativist commitments that lie beneath even the most committed relativist's worldview. Adapted from Rokeach's terminal vs. instrumental values research.

**Background:** Milton Rokeach's research on values (1973) demonstrated that human values can be usefully divided into *terminal values* (end states: what you want your life, your community, and the world to ultimately be like) and *instrumental values* (means: how you want to act in pursuit of those terminal values). What is most revealing about Rokeach's research is that under conditions of genuine choice — when values are brought into real conflict — the rank-ordering of values becomes unavoidable. You cannot simultaneously act on two values that require contradictory actions; you must, in practice, treat one as more fundamental.

The title of this practice names the ultimate version of this. What would you literally be willing to die for — or, less dramatically, to lose something significant over, to risk your reputation or relationships for, to stand against significant opposition for?

The hypothesis of this practice: beneath even the most thoroughgoing relativism lies a set of terminal values that are held as unconditionally, not relatively, valuable. These values constitute the non-relativist foundation of the relativist worldview. Identifying them is not a refutation of pluralism. It is an honest accounting of what pluralism is actually built on — and a first step toward a more coherent relationship to hierarchy.

**Instructions:**

**Part 1: Unconstrained articulation (10 minutes)**

Without filtering for consistency with your stated epistemological commitments, write your answers to these questions:

- What outcomes in the world matter enough to you that you would make significant personal sacrifices to achieve or prevent them?
- What values, if consistently violated in a community you were part of, would cause you to leave that community — or to resist?
- What is the difference, for you, between "a perspective I disagree with but accept as equally valid" and "a perspective that crosses a line"? Where is that line, and why does it exist?
- Finish this sentence: "I do not care how culturally embedded this perspective is or how sincerely it is held — I will not accept _____ as equally valid to its opposite."

**Part 2: Name the hierarchy (10 minutes)**

Looking at what you have written: your answers reveal a set of values that you hold as not merely equally valid, but as genuinely better than their negations. You are holding a hierarchy — a set of things that matter more than other things.

For each terminal value you have identified, write explicitly: "I hold [value X] as genuinely better than its opposite, and this is not merely a cultural preference — I believe this distinction tracks something real."

Notice what this feels like to write. You may find resistance — a voice that says "but who am I to judge?" Notice that voice. It is the performative contradiction in action.

**Part 3: Ground your pluralism (10–15 minutes)**

Now the crucial move: if you hold some values as genuinely better than their opposites, you are implicitly committed to the existence of *some* meta-perspective that makes this judgment possible. You are not a pure relativist. You are a person who holds certain things as genuinely valuable — and who extends tolerance to many perspectives while maintaining non-negotiable commitments in a smaller set of core cases.

This is the beginning of Integral perspectivism: a position that can say *both* "multiple perspectives are valid and worth taking seriously" *and* "some perspectives are more comprehensive, more inclusive, more coherent than others — and that distinction matters."

Reflect:
1. What is the relationship between your non-negotiable values and your pluralistic commitments? Are they in conflict, or can you see them as operating at different levels?
2. What would a more honest and coherent articulation of your actual position look like? Not "all perspectives are equally valid" — but something more precise, that can acknowledge both your genuine pluralism and your genuine hierarchy of values?

## Pre/Trans Checkpoint: Recognizing Green's Limits vs. Dismissing Green

<Admonition type="warning" title="Pre/Trans Checkpoint">

The recognition of Green's performative contradiction can be used in two fundamentally different ways, and the distinction between them matters enormously.

**Genuine recognition (trans-Green, humble, expansive)** looks like this:
- A felt sense of something genuinely hard being acknowledged — there is intellectual discomfort, perhaps some vulnerability, but also curiosity and even a kind of relief: "I have been feeling this tension for a long time and now I can name it."
- Continued respect and gratitude for Green's genuine contributions — the recognition of Green's ceiling does not flatten Green's floor. Green's pluralism, its inclusion of marginalized perspectives, its care for the web of relationships and ecology — these remain essential and are not undone by the acknowledgment of Green's epistemological limitation.
- An opening rather than a closing — the question becomes "what comes next?" rather than "I am done with this stage." The orientation is forward and curious, not superior or dismissive.
- A recognition that the *person* who sees Green's limitation is not thereby beyond Green. Seeing a stage's ceiling intellectually is not the same as having stabilized a more complex center of gravity. This module is not a graduation certificate.

**Dismissing Green (shadow of premature Integral) looks like this:**
- A sense of relief that is primarily competitive or distancing — "I knew Green was wrong; this confirms it." The recognition of Green's limitation becomes ammunition in an existing argument rather than genuine developmental insight.
- Loss of respect for Green's genuine contributions — "Green is just another stage to move past." This is what Wilber identifies as one of Integral's worst shadows: a subtle, intellectualized contempt for Green's concerns that mistakes the ability to see Green's ceiling for permission to dismiss Green's floor.
- Using the performative-contradiction argument as a conversation-stopper in mixed-stage environments — deploying philosophical sophistication as a form of hierarchical dominance rather than genuine dialogue. This is not Integral clarity; it is the dominator hierarchy Green correctly feared, now wearing Integral clothes.
- Thinking that recognizing Green's epistemological limitation means that Green's *values* (care, inclusion, ecological consciousness, relational attunement) are less important. They are not. Integral perspectivism holds these values *more* coherently than Green relativism can, precisely because it can ground them.

**The discriminating question:** Does your recognition of Green's limits make you *more* able to appreciate what Green actually achieves, or does it make you feel *above* it? Genuine developmental movement always includes the prior stage with greater comprehension and compassion. If the recognition produces contempt, it is not Integral — it is a new form of in-group superiority.

</Admonition>

## The Integral Opening

When the performative contradiction of radical pluralism is genuinely recognized — not as a defeat but as information — it opens a question that Green's epistemology could not raise: *What would a position look like that could affirm genuine pluralism without collapsing into relativism?*

The beginning of an answer: a position that can hold **both** of the following simultaneously:
- Multiple perspectives capture real aspects of reality and deserve genuine engagement. No perspective is simply wrong; every perspective illuminates something that others miss. The appropriate response to genuine difference is curiosity, not dismissal.
- Some perspectives are more comprehensive, more inclusive, and more internally coherent than others. This distinction is real, valuable, and provides the ground for evaluation, action, and development. It does not require domination; it does not negate the genuine contributions of less comprehensive perspectives.

This is the move that Wilber calls **integral perspectivism** (or second-tier thinking in Spiral Dynamics terms). It does not abandon Green's pluralism; it provides the epistemological ground that makes pluralism coherent. The pluralism becomes *grounded pluralism* rather than *groundless relativism*.

## 🌱 Embody

Recognizing Green's limits does not consolidate a new developmental center of gravity. What consolidates a new center of gravity is living from it — making decisions, engaging with people, and working through conflicts from a place that can hold both pluralistic appreciation and discerning evaluation.

**The commitment format:**

Complete this sentence:

*"In the next two weeks, I will apply integral perspectivism — the capacity to say 'this perspective is genuine and limited' rather than either 'this perspective is wrong' or 'this perspective is equally valid to all others' — to [specific situation: a conflict, a decision, a relationship, a political or organizational context]. The moment I am most likely to default to pure relativism ('who am I to judge?') is [specific trigger]. The moment I am most likely to default to premature dismissal ('this perspective is clearly inferior') is [different trigger]. My preparation is [specific practice: re-reading the three-positions section; completing the hierarchy map; returning to the 'What Would You Die For?' reflection]."*

**Examples:**

- *"In the next two weeks, I will apply integral perspectivism to a conflict in my workplace about organizational structure. Colleagues with strong Green commitments are resisting the introduction of any formal hierarchy; colleagues with strong Orange commitments are insisting on clear accountability structures. My default is to take the Green position and dismiss the Orange concern. My preparation is to map both positions against the dominator/actualization hierarchy distinction before the next meeting."*
- *"In the next two weeks, I will bring integral perspectivism to a political disagreement I have been avoiding with a family member. My default is to become either relativistically silent ('who am I to impose my values?') or to dismiss their view entirely. I will aim to hold both the genuine insight in their perspective and the specific ways in which I believe my own is more comprehensive — without needing to win the argument."*

:::note Safety Note

**Tier:** Tier 2 — moderate intensity. This module involves intellectually demanding philosophical self-examination that can produce significant identity discomfort. This can be experienced as destabilizing, particularly for learners who are strongly identified with their pluralistic values.

**Consent:** Proceed when you are willing to examine your own epistemological commitments honestly — including the possibility that your current worldview has genuine structural limitations. This is not an invitation to abandon your values. It is an invitation to hold them with greater honesty and coherence. You may pause or stop at any practice at any time. No response is evaluated externally.

**Contraindications:** If you are currently in a significant identity disruption or crisis of meaning, postpone this module and seek support from a trusted person or qualified practitioner. This module is not appropriate as an entry point to developmental inquiry; it presupposes familiarity with stage frameworks and genuine consolidation of Green values as a base. Do not use this module's philosophical arguments as weapons in ongoing relational or political conflicts — the intention is self-examination, not argumentation. This platform does not provide therapy, diagnosis, or clinical care.
